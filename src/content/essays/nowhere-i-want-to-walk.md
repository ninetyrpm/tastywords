I live in a shotgun house on a lot not much bigger than the house itself.

This is not what I used to imagine success looking like. I grew up in Beavercreek, Ohio, outside Dayton, where houses spread outward into what had once been cornfields and farms. It was the kind of place where a big house with a big yard could present itself not as one particular way of living, but as the obvious destination of adulthood. You went to school, got a good job, bought more space, accumulated nicer things, and moved somewhere safe enough that very little happened to you unexpectedly.

I absorbed most of this without consciously being taught it. I also learned something else about environments before I had much language for it. Beavercreek was predominantly white, and I looked Asian enough to be reminded regularly that I was not. I learned about race in textbooks, of course, but I also learned about it through jokes, bullying, physical aggression, and the quieter curriculum by which a child discovers that something about his face or family can become available for public commentary before he has decided what any of it means to him.

One adaptation was to make the joke first. If other people were going to notice the difference, I could try to control the noticing. I became fluent in self-deprecation. I could participate in my own caricature and pretend that doing so made me less vulnerable to it. I did not understand this at the time as shame or assimilation. I understood it as learning how to get along.

That was probably my first lesson in what environments do to deviation. They do not merely constrain behavior. They also teach us what belongs, sometimes before behavior enters the picture at all. If the surrounding norm possesses enough authority, standing out against it can feel less like evidence that the norm is narrow than evidence that you need correcting.

For a long time, I mostly assumed the task was to adapt myself to the environment.

College did little to disturb that assumption. Rose-Hulman gave me my first experience of living away from home, but it was an isolated campus surrounded by a place I had been conditioned to regard with some suspicion. Terre Haute was not exactly a throbbing center of cosmopolitan excitement, but my apprehension was about more than entertainment. I had grown up wary of urban interactions generally. Cities were places where things happened to you. Suburbs were places where you were protected from them.

After freshman year, I moved into a fraternity house on campus, which in retrospect feels almost comically predictable. Given a chance to venture farther into the surrounding world, I recreated the density and social familiarity of dorm life instead.

My first job took me to Anderson, Indiana, and Anderson complicated nearly everything I thought I understood about prosperity.

I was an engineer at Remy International, a company descended from the remains of an automotive industrial presence that had once shaped the town much more completely. Coworkers told me what Anderson had been like before jobs disappeared. They described a place that had depended heavily on one employer and then had to figure out what remained when that employer no longer supported the same kind of life.

That dependence had not always looked like vulnerability. For decades, a dominant industrial employer could provide exactly the kind of stability people reasonably wanted: good jobs, predictable incomes, a reason to build a life in one place. The same concentration that made that stability possible also meant there were fewer equivalent places to turn when it receded. A system can feel remarkably secure while it is working and reveal how few alternatives survived around it only after it stops.

I was making a comfortable engineering salary and living in one of the cheapest places I had ever encountered. At first, I understood that as a benefit. My money went far. Housing was inexpensive. I could look at Anderson through the familiar phrase *low cost of living* and see an advantage.

It took hearing other people’s stories to understand how different the same fact could look from the other side. What I experienced as low cost of living, someone else could experience as lack of economic mobility. Cheap housing is not particularly liberating if it is cheap partly because the jobs that once sustained the community have vanished. Several of the hourly technicians I worked with possessed knowledge and practical ability I genuinely admired, yet still struggled financially in ways I did not. Where I saw affordability, they could see a place where the exits had narrowed.

Even the townhouse I rented seemed to contain some of that history. It had been rebuilt after a fire, an attempt to make something livable again from literal ashes. That may be too neat a metaphor, but Anderson was the first place where I really encountered the possibility that an environment could be shaped by forces much larger than the people trying to live within it, and that the same environment could offer radically different possibilities depending on the position from which you entered it.

I eventually moved to Fishers, Indiana, which felt more like a return to the world I knew. Fishers was safe, comfortable, prosperous and thoroughly suburban. My girlfriend, now my wife, and I lived first in an apartment and later in a rented detached house. The latter felt like progress: three bedrooms, no stranger living on the other side of the wall, a yard, more privacy, more of our own.

This was also where I started riding bicycles.

At first, even cycling fit comfortably inside the suburban arrangement. I would put the bike in or on the car, drive somewhere, and begin the ride there. The bicycle was recreational equipment rather than transportation. The roads connected destinations. The interesting part started after I had driven to the place where I intended to be.

Then our landlord sold the house and we had to move quickly. We ended up in a one-bedroom apartment in Carmel.

On paper, this was a fairly obvious regression. We had gone from a three-bedroom detached home to shared walls and very little space. But the apartment, Mezz 42, was part of Carmel’s deliberate attempt to manufacture something resembling a more urban, even vaguely European environment inside one of America’s more affluent suburbs. It was not exactly Paris. It was still Carmel. But the Monon Trail ran nearby. Restaurants we loved were within walking distance. We could leave the apartment and encounter things without first deciding they were important enough to justify a drive.

I began to evaluate places partly by bicycle. This is not a particularly rigorous metric. Municipal planners have more respectable measures for density, access, land use and transportation. I had a bike and increasingly cared about whether I could get coffee without putting on real pants and starting a car.

The bicycle changed the geometry of the place. A car gave me much greater range, but it encouraged me to experience the world as a collection of destinations separated by space worth traversing as efficiently as possible. On a bicycle, the space between destinations became inhabitable. I noticed the alley, the strange building, the shortcut, the business I had never seen before. I could change direction because something looked interesting. Curiosity became cheap.

That changed how I understood the apartment too. It contained less private territory than the detached house, but my life no longer had to fit entirely inside it.

> Space and access are not the same thing.

 A large house could contain nearly everything I owned while leaving almost everything I wanted to do several miles away. A smaller home could feel strangely expansive if the world began just outside the door.

Eventually, of course, we moved back into another detached house in Carmel. Once you have spent enough of your life understanding bigger as better, the attractions remain easy to measure. More bedrooms. More storage. More separation. The advantages of proximity are harder to put into a listing.

That was the house I was living in when I got fired.

The firing hurt in a way I was not prepared for. My behavior, working methods and principles did not align with what my employer expected of me. At the time, I did not have a particularly sophisticated way of separating that judgment from a judgment of my worth. I had been hired as an engineer by a successful company. The company had now determined that it no longer wanted me. The easiest conclusion was that I had failed.

Some of the criticism was deserved. I showed up late regularly without ever really negotiating for the flexibility I apparently believed I should have. I sometimes took the perks around me for granted. I had developed an instinctive resistance to corporate rituals and hierarchy, and although I did not intentionally arrive at work waving an anti-corporate flag, I suspect it was more visible than I imagined. I made choices. Those choices had consequences.

Other parts of the environment were harder to interpret as lessons I simply needed to learn. Our engineering director repeatedly called me by the name of another Asian colleague, even after being corrected. We did not look alike. The mistake became a source of jokes downstream, including some I participated in at my own expense because I had learned long before that joining the joke was easier than explaining why it bothered me.

I do not tell that story because I think it explains why I was fired. I do not think it does. I tell it because it captures what I could not disentangle at the time. Some expectations I resisted probably deserved more respect than I gave them. Some of my behavior needed to change. And some things I had learned to accommodate in the environment were not evidence of my own deficiency at all.

I could not yet separate those categories cleanly. Was I resisting expectations because the expectations were unreasonable, or because I was immature? Was I a poor fit, or was the place itself a poor fit for me? Was I insufficiently disciplined? Was I insufficiently compliant? Which criticisms should I learn from, and which should I reject? Beavercreek had taught me how quickly difference could become something I managed inside myself. Work had now added behavior, authority, professionalism and economic dependence to the equation.

Being fired compressed all of those questions into one answer: *you are the problem.*

It took me a while to recover enough to notice the gift hidden inside the violence of that simplification. Nothing tied me to Indiana anymore.

So I opened the map.

I interviewed in New York, Detroit, Baton Rouge, Cincinnati, California and elsewhere. I considered places I found exciting and places I found considerably less exciting. After growing up in Ohio, going to college in Indiana and beginning my career in Indiana, I suddenly had permission to imagine that geography itself was negotiable.

Naturally, after opening my job search to most of the United States, I moved to Kentucky.

The job was with GE Appliances in Louisville. We rented a house in Deer Park. Louisville was not New York or Chicago. Deer Park was not downtown. But the neighborhood offered something I had only partially experienced in Carmel. Restaurants, parks, shops, people and streets belonged to the same environment as the house rather than being separate destinations reached from it.

The bicycle changed too. In Fishers, I had often driven somewhere to ride. In Louisville, riding became a way of encountering Louisville itself. The city was messier than the places I had grown up imagining as ideal, and that was part of the appeal. There was noise, traffic, conflict, people unlike me, people doing things I would not have chosen if I had been given authority over the block. Urban life did not maximize my control over my surroundings. It exposed me to more of them.

Eventually another landlord displaced us. His divorce meant the Deer Park house was no longer ours to rent, which was irritating enough that we finally bought something.

We bought the shotgun house in Clifton.

The house would have looked absurdly small compared with what my younger self assumed I was working toward. The lot would not impress anyone shopping for acreage. Some of the houses I grew up around probably have garages approaching the square footage of our first floor.

But by then I had stopped thinking of the property line as the boundary of my home. The neighborhood was part of what I was buying.

Around roughly the same period, something similar happened at work. I moved from GE Appliances proper to FirstBuild.

FirstBuild is difficult to explain cleanly because the things that make it unusual tend to sound like perks when listed individually. There is a makerspace. The public can come in. We build prototypes. We launch products. People move across disciplines. The organization is small.

None of those facts quite captures it. The more important difference is that the distance between things is short.

An engineer can get involved in product strategy. Someone running a program can build a software tool. A member of the public can walk into the same building where products are being developed. A strange idea can move from conversation to prototype before it has acquired enough paperwork to become an official idea. People do work outside their expertise because sometimes the useful question is not whose responsibility something is, but whether it needs doing.

That does not always work beautifully. Things fall through cracks. People duplicate effort. Under-resourcing can masquerade as empowerment. A capable employee can become the heroic patch over an organizational deficiency, donating invisible labor until everyone forgets the hole underneath. Recognition can be uneven. There is not much of a corporate ladder, which is one reason some people eventually leave.

And yet I found myself more willing to do thankless work there than I ever had inside a larger organization. Part of the reason is embarrassingly simple: I can often see what happens because I did it.

The distance between effort and consequence is short too. If I build a tool that helps my team, I can watch them use it. If I help move a product forward, I know the people whose problem it solves. If something in the building needs doing and I do it, the result usually does not disappear upward into an abstraction called organizational performance.

> The work feels less like contribution to a system and more like participation in a place.

That distinction changed how I interpreted my earlier career. The traits that made me troublesome in one environment did not disappear when I arrived at FirstBuild. I did not finally become punctual, deferential, narrowly specialized or especially interested in staying inside my lane. I still had to mature. I still had to learn when resistance was principled and when it was simply resistance. But some of those same traits became useful.

For the first time, I could see how much of my previous discomfort had come from trying to make myself compatible with an arrangement I had never thought to question. I had spent years assuming that discomfort was evidence something was wrong with me. Then I found environments in which substantially the same person could be interpreted differently.

That realization is comforting only if you stop there.

Environments do not merely reward or punish traits. They change the friction around them and help determine which become legible as strengths, which become liabilities, and which never get enough room to appear. A bicycle is not inherently impractical. Put it on a six-lane arterial with no shoulder and it becomes impractical very quickly. A person inclined to wander across disciplines is not inherently innovative or insubordinate. The surrounding structure changes the odds of which interpretation will stick.

I began to notice one complication when I started supervising engineering co-ops. By then I had accumulated enough standing at FirstBuild that I could arrive at nine without much apology, disappear from my desk because I was making something, or spend time on a problem nobody had formally assigned to me. Usually those behaviors were interpreted through the accumulated evidence around them: experience, judgment, initiative, past performance.

Then I imagined a twenty-year-old co-op doing the same thing. Would wandering look like curiosity or lack of focus? Would arriving later look like flexibility or laziness? The actions could be identical while the interpretation differed.

Some of the freedom I experience really does belong to FirstBuild’s culture, and some of it belongs to the position I have accumulated inside that culture. Seniority, trust and reputation give ambiguity a way of resolving in my favor. People do not enter an environment with equal presumptions of competence, belonging, or good intent, and removing a formal boundary does not automatically remove that difference.

That creates an obligation. If I value an environment in which people are allowed discretion, I cannot simply enjoy the discretion I have earned and call the culture free. I have to help newer people distinguish boundaries that protect something important from habits that merely look official, make it safer for them to test an edge before they have enough status to survive being wrong about one, and extend some of the interpretive generosity I receive to people who have not yet accumulated enough credibility to demand it.

> A permeable environment still needs to be navigable by someone who does not already possess the map.

Otherwise, what looks like autonomy from where I stand can become a collection of privileges available mostly to people who already know the unwritten rules.

This is also why I have become less interested in the easy version of the argument in which structure is the enemy. Large organizations do not invent roles, processes, metrics and formal authority solely because executives enjoy ruining everyone’s afternoon. A role can tell a junior employee what is actually expected instead of leaving them vulnerable to whatever a more powerful person decides to ask that day. A documented process can preserve institutional memory. A metric can expose favoritism that might otherwise hide comfortably inside someone’s confidence in their own judgment. Informality can be generous, but it can also be brutal to people who do not resemble, know, or intuitively understand the people already holding power.

Formal systems never eliminate informal judgment. Someone still has to notice when the written process does not fit the problem, when two categories overlap, or when an exception matters. Sometimes the person crossing the boundary is not working around the system so much as quietly making it work.

I do not want every wall torn down. I want to be able to cross some of them.

Organizations can sprawl just as neighborhoods can. A boundary becomes a role. The role becomes a function. The function acquires a process. The process acquires an approval. The approval acquires a metric. Each addition can solve a real problem.

The danger comes from accumulation. A neighborhood does not become unwalkable because someone wakes up one morning and bans walking. A road gets widened. A destination moves farther away. A parking lot grows. A crossing disappears. Each decision can be defended individually until eventually the easiest way to participate in ordinary life is to get into a car.

An organization can reach the same condition. The person who once could notice something and act has to determine who owns it, justify the crossing, find a budget, obtain approval and translate the result into something legible to people increasingly distant from the work itself. The company may contain thousands of possibilities. They are simply far apart.

That is the corporate version of the suburban promise: enormous capacity coupled with surprisingly little access.

FirstBuild has historically felt different because so many of those distances remained short. That is also what makes me afraid for it.

For most of my childhood, I had understood suburban life as something close to a natural outcome of prosperity. I know now that the landscape I inherited did not simply emerge from millions of families independently discovering the same preference. Mortgage policy, zoning, highways, racial exclusion, development economics and cultural ideas about property all helped decide which forms of American life became easy to finance, legal to build, and recognizable as success. Individual preferences mattered, but they were exercised inside a system that had already made some choices much easier than others.

Corporate life develops defaults of its own. Education teaches us recognizable professions. Recruiting funnels us into roles. Titles make status legible. Compensation systems reward certain kinds of advancement. Promotion ladders tell us which direction counts as up. Professional norms tell us what serious work is supposed to look like.

The alternative then has to explain itself. The suburb is normal; the dense neighborhood is a lifestyle. The large corporation is professional; the small experimental organization is weird. A promotion is ambition; moving sideways is lack of focus.

Alternatives can be threatening simply because they demonstrate that the dominant arrangement was never inevitable. A place like FirstBuild only has to survive long enough to make certain assumptions look optional. If a small team can develop products without all the expected boundaries, then some of those boundaries become questions rather than laws.

Once a rule becomes recognizable as a choice, it has to defend itself in a way that nature never did.

So alternatives get pressured to become more legible, governable, measurable, aligned and controlled. Not necessarily because anyone has decided to destroy them. A parent company wants comparable budgets. A profession develops standards. A successful experiment wants to scale. Leaders want fewer surprises. People copy structures that other respectable organizations already use because doing so is safer than inventing governance from scratch.

Many of those pressures are reasonable. FirstBuild should have accountability. It should manage risk. It should know where money goes. It should not rely indefinitely on heroic people covering invisible gaps.

But I become nervous when every ambiguity is treated as a deficiency waiting to be fixed. The qualities being removed may include the ones that made the place capable of something unusual. An organization that eliminates every inconvenience of permeability eventually starts eliminating permeability itself.

None of this means inconvenience is virtuous. I do not prefer the city because noise is character-building, or the bicycle because weather and traffic make suffering more authentic. I do not think FirstBuild becomes better every time someone compensates for missing resources or unclear responsibility. Some friction is simply dysfunction.

What I value is the friction attached to participation rather than the friction attached to exclusion. Living near other people requires negotiation because other people are actually present. Crossing disciplines creates ambiguity because more than one perspective is allowed into the same problem. The harder path is not better because it is harder. It is sometimes worth the difficulty because of what the difficulty keeps within reach.

This is not an argument that everyone should choose the same alternative I did. Plenty of people genuinely prefer more private space, quieter neighborhoods, clearer responsibilities, predictable advancement and defined boundaries around what they owe an employer. Cities require negotiation. Small organizations can be exhausting. Ambiguity is not equally energizing to everyone.

But I no longer believe our choices take place on neutral ground.

For much of my life, I did not reject urban living after carefully comparing it with suburban living. I barely understood the alternative. The same is true of work. I knew what a successful career was supposed to look like long before I knew there were organizations where the ladder itself might be the wrong unit of measurement.

And even once an alternative becomes visible, not everyone can choose it. Anderson taught me that before I understood what I was learning. I have been fortunate enough to change jobs, cities and homes. I was able to respond to being fired by searching nationally. Eventually I could buy a house in a neighborhood I preferred. I can resist certain workplace conventions partly because I have accumulated enough experience and economic security to survive doing so.

Many people cannot simply decide that the environment is the problem and leave. A mortgage, healthcare, children, geography, immigration status, specialized skills, money, family obligations or a collapsed local labor market can turn an arrangement someone dislikes into the only realistic one available.

You cannot meaningfully choose an alternative you cannot see, cannot afford, or cannot reach.

This is why my fear of someday leaving FirstBuild is not primarily fear of being unemployable. I am not especially worried about finding another job. I have accumulated enough strange experience that I am confident I can explain my value to other employers.

I worry about whether I would want to work for them.

FirstBuild has made conventional employment harder for me to imagine for the same reason Clifton has made a distant subdivision harder for me to imagine. I know I could live there. I understand the comforts. I can see the appeal of the large house, the large yard, the cleanly defined role, the prestigious employer, the promotion ladder and the carefully managed career.

I wanted those things once. Now I know what they can cost.

That knowledge has made me more willing to act on what I value rather than wait for an institution to validate it. I race bicycles, help organize events, build tools and websites, and take on work because I think something should exist or because I can see how making it might help. I have become less interested in waiting to be appointed before participating.

That is not the same as believing permission, consent or responsibility no longer matter. It means I no longer assume that the absence of permission is evidence that I should not act.

Maybe I discovered a new theory of freedom somewhere along the way. If so, it is much less glamorous than the word suggests. I spent much of my life pursuing larger enclosures and eventually discovered that what I wanted was more ways to move beyond them.

The small house gave me more neighborhood. The bicycle gave me more city. The smaller organization gave me more ways to be useful.

None of those environments is a utopia. The city can be dirty, unequal, bureaucratic and exhausting. The bicycle is vulnerable and occasionally miserable. FirstBuild can be chaotic, under-resourced, inconsistent and maddening.

But the underlying arrangement permits a kind of life the alternatives often suppress.

That is what makes losing it frightening.

I have literally wondered what it would take to buy FirstBuild. The thought is ridiculous for several reasons, not least of which is that buying an organization in order to preserve everyone else’s freedom contains exactly the kind of contradiction that should make a person nervous.

But I understand the impulse.

When we were displaced from Deer Park, I eventually solved the problem by buying the little house in Clifton. Ownership gave me some durable claim to the environment I had finally learned I wanted.


> There is no comparable deed for a way of working.

Leadership can change. Strategy can change. The parent company can change. The culture can slowly become something else. I can leave voluntarily or be asked to leave involuntarily. Even if FirstBuild survives, the particular conditions that taught me what I value may not.

For the first time, I understand that fear clearly. It is not the fear that no one else will hire me. It is the fear of knowing exactly what I would be giving up if they did.

Perhaps the next move will be chosen. Perhaps I will recognize the enclosure before it becomes intolerable. Perhaps I will find another place where the distances between interesting things remain short.

Or perhaps it will be another forced displacement.

Only this time, I may know enough to build somewhere to go.
