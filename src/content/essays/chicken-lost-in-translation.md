I am sitting on a stool. It's not a particularly comfortable or attractive stool. It wobbles slightly, and the vinyl cushion is patched with duct tape the color of an old bruise. However, this stool is, without argument, one of the best seats I will occupy all year.

In front of me is a trough of glowing binchotan, throwing heat like a small, well-behaved sun. Behind that wall of heat is a man who has spent over forty years learning to tame that fire and master exactly when to salt a chicken thigh at the perfect altitude above the coals. To the uninitiated, it might seem like an artform. To those in the know, the man's uninterested precision is more like shuffling a deck of cards; it is something performed with intuition and reflex. The man behind the grill is not performing. The theater he provides is merely incidental — a side effect of the kitchen being roughly the size of a phone booth and located, out of simple necessity, three feet from your face.

Here, at this restaurant, you don't order a dinner. You order a skewer, or two. Then twelve minutes later, admittedly somewhat powerlessly, you order another. The pacing of the meal is dictated less by pre-planned courses and more by the alternating rounds of umami deliciousness and ice cold beer or whiskey highball.

I've come to the somber conclusion that this restaurant format, and more importantly, the experience provided by it, will never make its way properly to the United States. And the reason has almost nothing to do with chicken, and almost everything to do with the fact that you'd have to drive there.

---

Yakitori-ya, the tiny grilled-chicken counters of Japan, run on an absurdly specific set of inputs: a footprint under 300 square feet in a neighborhood dense enough to walk to, with an owner-operator who is simultaneously the busser, chef, and HR department. Most importantly, and the part nobody wants to hear, is the absence of the assumption that the restaurant needs a parking lot.

Japan's own labor statistics make the skeleton quite visible. Over 80% of the country's eating and drinking establishments run with fewer than ten employees. That's not a charming cultural footnote about humble artisans. That is culinary infrastructure. It doesn't need to be anyone's ambition, growth plan, or grind. It's a business that can afford to serve gizzards to nine people a night in perpetuity, because nine people a night is the whole plan. It was never supposed to be more than that.

Now do the American math. The National Restaurant Association's own numbers show labor alone eating 36.5% of revenue at the median full-service restaurant before you've even paid rent. An eleven-seat chicken-parts restaurant is not viable in that environment. It often survives (if it survives at all) on something closer to charity, hype, or a founder eating the losses out of love for the craft.

So the restaurant does the only rational thing a cornered organism can do: it grows. It adds the entrée. It adds the vegetarian option, the lunch menu, the party platter, and the thing on page four that isn't yakitori but pays the rent that yakitori can't. Every single decision in that chain is sensible. That's the tragedy. Nobody in this story is an idiot, and nobody's chasing a bad idea. The system just quietly punishes the good one.

---

A guy leaving Yūrakuchō station at 7:40PM on an ordinary Tuesday can pass upwards of a dozen viable, entirely different dinners in the four hundred meters that separate the train platform and his front door: a ramen counter, a standing bar, a place that serves nothing but eel. He didn't plan anny of it. The city simply handed him a menu made of doors.

Meanwhile, leaving your office in literally any American city (at least other than one of the three major ones we keep exhaustedly pointing to) is a different experience. You are also passing a bunch of potential dinners on the way home, except you're passing them by at forty-five miles an hour, sealed inside a two-ton box on wheels. You never just wander into dinner. You commit to it, and by the time you've found parking, you've already decided that this had better be worth it.

This isn't merely a small culutural difference. It is entirely the explanation. A parking lot doesn't create diners. It creates destinations, ones that come loaded with expectations that chicken hearts and livers were never meant to satisfy. Nobody drives twenty minutes, hunts for parking, and feeds the meter to eat modestly. The car doesn't just get us there. It also rewires our brains in defining what "there" is allowed to be.

This is the unglamorous, unsexy, spreadsheet-and-zoning-map truth sitting underneath every wistful essay about how Americans "just don't get" small-plate dining. We get it fine. But we built a country where getting it costs you a forty-five-minute round trip and a parking validation. Car-dependency didn't just change how we move. It changed what kind of restaurant is economically sane to open, because it decided, on our behalf, that every restaurant now has to be worth the drive.

---

American commercial real estate increasingly prices space for the tenant who can guarantee rent, not the tenant with the most interesting idea. That ideal tenant is often a chain. Chains can absorb a bad month. Chains have a regional manager who signs a five-year lease without losing sleep. The guy who wants to sell chicken hearts to nine strangers a night has none of that collateral. His decade of culinary technique and small business plan read to a leasing office like a financial liability.

So the small, specific, single-obsession restaurant gets quietly priced out of exactly the kind of walkable corridor that could've sustained it, replaced by the fourth regional taco concept this year, which isn't a knock on tacos. Rather, it is a criticism of the fact that American zoning and lending both prefer the legible, franchisable, parkable version of a thing to the seemingly strange, yet authentic version of it. Japan's zoning, by contrast, still lets tiny commercial spaces bleed directly into residential blocks: shops sheltered under apartments and restaurants sized like closets. Its easy for American tourists to interpret these tucked away eateries as hidden gem novelties, but for locals, its everyday life.

---

Someone reading this bound to argue that the U.S. does indeed have yakitori, and they are bound to point to one of two polar examples. America solved the yakitori problem the way America solves most imported things it doesn't know what to do with: it built two versions, and stranded the truth in between them.

Version one looks something like a chicken-on-a-stick at your neighborhood pan-Asian spot: smothered in teriyaki sauce, costs seven bucks, tastes utterly fine, and buried in a menu entirely unrelated to the thing it's named after. Nothing wrong with it. It's just a costume.

Version two is usually embodied by the $185 omakase counter. Sure, it's got eight seats, but it came with a reservation you fought a small war to obtain. Meanwhile, skewers like chicken cartilage are presented with the gravity of a papal announcement. This version is aeshtetically gorgeous, but it is also, structurally, a magic trick — it takes everything that would make version one feel confusing at a normal price ("wait, we're eating the skin?") and turns it into the entire selling point once you've paid enough to be excited about it instead of suspicious of it. Money doesn't just buy the ingredients. It buys your willingness to be told this is good.

What's missing is the boring, glorious middle. Serious but unpretentious, and cheap enough to dine on a Tuesday. I'm talking about the king of place you go because you're tired, not because you're celebrating. That version can't survive in America because it requires the one thing our restaurant economics can't manufacture on command: a neighborhood that already agrees, without being sold on it, that eleven seats and some bites of chicken tail are enough.

---

I'm not sad about the chicken, or even the physical restaurant that represents it. We have great chickens, we have binchotan shipped in by the crate, and we have talented chefs who know exactly what they're doing with a wing tip.

I'm sitting on that wobbly stool, twelve time zones away from my actual life, mourning the fact that the very version of commerce that makes the experience I'm currently living completely unviable back home. I'm mourning a country that built its cities around roads, rather than roads around its cities.
