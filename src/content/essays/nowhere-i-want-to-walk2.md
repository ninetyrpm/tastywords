# I Priced Out Buying the Company I Work For

I once did the math on what it would cost to buy the company I work for.

I couldn't afford it, not by a wide margin, and the idea barely survives contact with common sense — buying an organization in order to protect everyone else's freedom is its own small contradiction. But I ran the number anyway, because I couldn't think of another way to make sure nobody could take the place away from me.

That impulse only makes sense against a lifetime of assuming I wanted the opposite of whatever turned out to matter. I grew up believing that adulthood meant accumulating space — a bigger house, a better title, a career with a visible ladder — and it took losing a job, buying a small house on a small lot, and getting around by bicycle before I understood I'd been chasing the wrong variable the whole time.

I didn't grow up believing any of that. I grew up in Beavercreek, Ohio, outside Dayton, where houses spread into what had recently been cornfields, and where a big house with a big yard wasn't one way of living among several — it was just what adulthood looked like when you did it right. You went to school, got a good job, bought more space, moved somewhere safe enough that little happened to you unexpectedly.

I also learned something else there, earlier than I had language for it. Beavercreek was mostly white, and I looked Asian enough to be reminded of it regularly — in jokes, in shoves, in the quieter curriculum by which a kid learns his face is available for public comment before he's decided what it means to him. One adaptation was to make the joke first. If people were going to notice, I could try to control the noticing. I got fluent in self-deprecation and told myself that was just how you got along. I'll come back to this, because it turns out unequal access isn't only about money.

College didn't disturb much. Rose-Hulman was my first time away from home, but it sat in a town — Terre Haute — I'd already been taught to regard as slightly dangerous, the way I'd been taught cities generally were places where things happened to you and suburbs were places where they didn't. Given the chance to explore, I moved into a fraternity house instead and rebuilt dorm life on purpose.

My first job did more to unsettle things. I was an engineer at Remy International in Anderson, Indiana, a company built from the remains of an automotive industry that used to run the whole town. Coworkers told me what Anderson had been before the jobs left — how one employer had provided exactly the stability people wanted for decades, and how that same concentration meant there was nowhere obvious to turn once it receded. Security can look complete right up until the moment it stops, and only then do you see how few alternatives were left standing around it.

I was earning a comfortable salary in one of the cheapest places I'd ever lived, and at first I read that as pure upside — my money went far. It took hearing other people's stories to see the same fact from the other side. What I experienced as low cost of living, several of the technicians I worked with — people with real skill and ability I admired — experienced as a lack of anywhere to go. Cheap housing isn't liberating if it's cheap because the jobs that once supported it left first. Anderson was the first place I saw that an environment isn't neutral ground: it can hand two people wildly different odds depending on where they enter it.

I moved next to Fishers, Indiana — safe, prosperous, thoroughly suburban, a return to the world I already understood. My girlfriend, now my wife, and I eventually rented a detached house there: three bedrooms, no shared wall, a yard. It felt like progress.

That's also where I started riding bicycles, though at first the bike barely counted as transportation. I'd drive somewhere, unload it, and ride from there — recreational equipment, not a way of getting anywhere.

Then our landlord sold the house out from under us, and we ended up, on short notice, in a one-bedroom apartment in Carmel. On paper this was a step down. In practice it was the first time I understood the difference that would end up mattering most to me. The apartment was part of Carmel's deliberate attempt to build something like a walkable European quarter inside one of America's wealthiest suburbs — not exactly Paris, but the Monon Trail ran nearby, restaurants were in walking distance, and I could leave the building and run into things without first deciding they were worth a drive.

I started judging places by whether I could get coffee without putting on real pants and starting a car, which is not a metric any city planner would respect but which changed how I moved through the world. A car gives you range, but it turns the world into destinations connected by distance worth minimizing. A bicycle makes the space between destinations inhabitable — you notice the alley, the odd building, the shortcut, and you can change direction because something looks interesting. Curiosity gets cheap.

> Space and access are not the same thing.

A big house can hold nearly everything you own while leaving almost everything you want to do several miles away. A small apartment can feel enormous if the world starts right outside the door.

We eventually moved back into a detached house in Carmel — more bedrooms, more storage, more separation, the kind of upgrade that's easy to put in a listing. That was the house I was living in when I got fired.

It hurt more than I expected, and for a while I didn't have a way to separate the judgment from a judgment of my worth. Some of it I'd earned: I showed up late without ever negotiating for the flexibility I assumed I deserved, and I'd developed a reflexive resistance to hierarchy that I doubt was as invisible as I imagined. But not everything I'd learned to absorb there was a lesson about my own deficiency. Our engineering director called me by another Asian colleague's name — repeatedly, even after correction — and the mistake became a running joke I sometimes joined at my own expense, because joining had always been easier than explaining why it bothered me.

I don't think that incident explains why I was fired. I think it explains why I couldn't tell, at the time, which criticisms to accept and which to reject. Was I resisting unreasonable expectations, or just being immature? Was the place a poor fit for me, or was I one for it? Being fired collapsed all of that into a single answer — *you are the problem* — and it took a while to notice the strange gift buried in that simplification: nothing tied me to Indiana anymore.

So I opened the map. I interviewed in New York, Detroit, Baton Rouge, Cincinnati, California — and, naturally, after opening my search to most of the country, I moved to Kentucky, to a job with GE Appliances in Louisville. We rented in Deer Park, a neighborhood where restaurants, parks, and people belonged to the same world as the house instead of being destinations reached from it. The bicycle changed accordingly — in Fishers I'd driven somewhere to ride; in Louisville, riding was how I met the city itself, mess and noise and all.

A second landlord's divorce eventually displaced us from Deer Park, which was irritating enough that we finally bought something: a shotgun house on a lot not much bigger than the house itself, in a neighborhood called Clifton. It would have looked absurd to my younger self — some houses I grew up around have garages close to its square footage — but by then I'd stopped treating the property line as the edge of my home. The neighborhood was part of what I was buying.

Around the same time, I moved from GE Appliances proper to FirstBuild — a small product-development shop with a public makerspace, where the distance between things is short. An engineer can wander into strategy. A visitor can walk into the room where a product is being built. An idea can go from conversation to prototype before it's acquired enough paperwork to be an official idea.

It doesn't always work. Things fall through cracks, and under-resourcing can dress itself up as empowerment — a capable person quietly patches the hole until everyone forgets it's there. But I've done more thankless work at FirstBuild than I ever did somewhere larger, for an embarrassingly simple reason: I can usually see what happens because I did it. If I build a tool, I watch my team use it. If something needs doing, the result doesn't disappear upward into an abstraction called organizational performance. It feels less like contributing to a system and more like taking part in a place.

That started to reframe my whole earlier career. I hadn't finally become punctual or deferential or good at staying in my lane — I still had many of the traits that had made me a poor fit there. They just meant something different here. An environment doesn't only reward or punish what you bring to it; it decides which parts of you become legible as a strength, which become a liability, and which never get enough room to show up at all.

But that comfort has a limit, and I found it when I started supervising engineering co-ops. By then I had enough standing at FirstBuild to arrive at nine without apology or disappear to build something nobody assigned me — and people read that through the evidence already stacked around me: experience, judgment, a track record. Then I imagined a twenty-year-old doing exactly the same thing. Does wandering read as curiosity, or as unfocus? Does arriving late read as flexibility, or as not caring? The action is identical. The interpretation isn't.

Some of my freedom belongs to FirstBuild's culture. Some of it belongs to the reputation I've built up inside that culture, and reputation buys ambiguity a way of resolving in your favor that it doesn't automatically extend to everyone.

> A permeable environment still needs to be navigable by someone who does not already possess the map.

Otherwise what looks like autonomy from where I stand is really a set of privileges available mainly to people who already know the unwritten rules. That obligation isn't abstract to me. The kid who learns to make the joke first isn't showing bad character — he's managing a room that offers him less benefit of the doubt than it offers other kids by default. If I want a workplace where discretion is real, I owe newer people — and especially the ones who don't walk in with an easy presumption of competence — some of the same generosity that seniority now hands me for free.

This is also why I've gone cold on the easy version of this argument, the one where structure is simply the enemy. Big organizations don't invent roles and metrics because executives enjoy paperwork. A defined role can protect a junior employee from whatever a more powerful person feels like demanding that day. A metric can expose favoritism that would otherwise hide inside somebody's confidence in their own judgment. Informality can be generous, but it can also be brutal to people who don't already resemble the people holding power. I don't want every wall gone. I want to be able to cross some of them.

Organizations sprawl the same way neighborhoods do — a boundary becomes a role, the role acquires a process, the process acquires an approval, and each step can be defended on its own even as the whole thing quietly turns into an organization with enormous capacity and very little access, the corporate version of the suburban promise. FirstBuild has stayed different mostly by keeping those distances short, which is exactly why I'm nervous about it. An alternative like this only has to survive long enough to make the usual assumptions look optional, and once a rule reveals itself as a choice rather than a law of nature, it has to start defending itself the way nature never did. So the pressure comes — for comparable budgets, common standards, fewer surprises — most of it reasonable in isolation, all of it capable, in sufficient quantity, of eliminating the very ambiguity that made the place unusual in the first place.

None of this is an argument that everyone should want what I want. Plenty of people genuinely prefer more private space, a quieter street, a clearer job description, a predictable ladder — ambiguity isn't energizing for everyone, and cities and small organizations both ask a lot of the people inside them.

But I no longer believe any of us choose from neutral ground, and I no longer believe we choose from equal ground either. I could respond to being fired by searching the whole country because I had savings, no dependents yet, and a resume that read as strength rather than instability. I could resist certain workplace conventions because I'd already banked enough credibility to survive being wrong about one. A mortgage, a health condition, children, immigration status, a collapsed local labor market, or the accumulated weight of never being given the benefit of the doubt in the first place can all turn an arrangement someone dislikes into the only one they can actually reach. You can't choose an alternative you can't see, can't afford, or were never shown belonged to you too. Access was never distributed evenly to begin with, and the people most constrained by an environment are often the least equipped to leave it.

So my fear about eventually leaving FirstBuild isn't about employability. I've collected enough strange experience to explain my value elsewhere without much trouble. What I'm actually afraid of is not wanting the places that would take me.

Buying the house in Clifton solved a version of this problem once before — ownership gave me a durable claim on an environment I'd finally learned I wanted.

> There is no comparable deed for a way of working.

Leadership changes. Strategy changes. A culture can drift into something else with nobody deciding to drift it there, and I can leave on my own terms or someone else's. Even if FirstBuild survives, the particular conditions that taught me any of this might not.

Maybe next time the move will be one I choose. Maybe I'll see the walls going up before they close. Or maybe it'll be another landlord's divorce, another firing, another forced relocation.

Either way, I think I finally know enough to build somewhere to go.
