I recently came across a Reddit thread in r/louisville about some local cycling personalities publicly tearing each other apart over what appeared to be the usual combination of egos, social media drama, and hurt feelings.

I wasn't entirely removed from the spectacle myself. Somewhere along the way, the apparently scandalous combination of being friends with someone involved and liking a comment had gotten me blocked and effectively excluded from a riding group. I hadn't even participated in the argument, but apparently I'd managed to pick a side.

One particular commenter, a self-described outsider to the cycling community, offered a devastatingly simple observation:

"You are riding around in spandex with clicky clacky shoes and your weiners out, doing an activity poor people and children use as a their main mode of transportation. I'm not going to yuck your yum but it's just riding a bike and everyone is making it too complicated."

I laughed my ass off.

Not because the observation was particularly original, but because there's something wonderfully humbling about having your entire subculture reduced to grown adults dressed like aerodynamic sausages, throwing tantrums over who gets to be in charge of riding bicycles. The sad thing is, they had a point. Some of us do take ourselves far too fucking seriously. But that bit about poor people and children deserves a second look.

I understand the sentiment. Riding a bicycle is a remarkably ordinary activity, and there's something absurd about grown adults manufacturing elaborate social hierarchies around it, but I couldn't help getting stuck on that particular comparison.

Why invoke poor people and children as evidence that something isn't worth taking so seriously? Why should the fact that children and people with limited means ride bicycles make the activity any less worthy of enthusiasm, dedication, or even serious competition? I think it's pretty remarkable that the same basic invention can get a kid to school, get someone to work, and give a grown man in Lycra an existential crisis over losing a Strava segment by three seconds. That's not what makes cycling ridiculous. That's what makes it wonderful.

The ridiculous part is what some of us manage to do with our egos. And before I sound like I'm observing all this from some comfortable moral distance, I should probably introduce myself.

I am precisely that ridiculous cyclist.

I'm a middle-aged man who voluntarily squeezes himself into skin-tight kit, spends embarrassing amounts of money on equipment that would be utterly incomprehensible to most people, and dedicates an unreasonable amount of his free time to making numbers on a screen go up.

I own a bicycle that costs more than some people's cars. I obsess over watts, aerodynamics, tire pressures, and components whose performance differences might amount to a handful of seconds over several hours. I chase imaginary trophies on a mobile app, often competing against strangers who have no idea they're participating in my personal vendetta.

And you know what, I fucking love it.

For years, though, I've had a complicated relationship with what all of that represents, primarily because I know the stereotype. The wealthy, usually white, middle-aged male professional with too much disposable income, a carbon fiber bicycle, and an inflated sense of his athletic prowess. The guy who thinks his $12,000 Pinarello makes him a professional cyclist, treats group rides like the Tour de France, and believes the rest of society should accommodate his very important hobby.

You've met him. I've met him. Sometimes he's wearing a jersey plastered with sponsors for a team nobody has ever heard of. Sometimes he's lecturing a woman about how to ride her bike, blissfully unaware that she could drop his ass on the next climb. Sometimes he's the guy who has appointed himself tsar of a bicycle club and somehow confused organizing a Saturday morning ride with governing a small sovereign nation.

For a long time, I was uncomfortable resembling him. I worried about what my equipment communicated about my privilege. What my competitive riding communicated about my ego. What riding quickly through the city communicated to someone who depends on a bicycle to get to work, or someone who has spent years advocating for safer streets. I wondered whether I was reinforcing an image of cycling that I found objectionable, even when I wasn't necessarily behaving in the ways that made it objectionable.

The more I learned about the culture of competitive cycling, the more complicated that relationship became. Road cycling has a long history of exclusion. It's a sport whose traditions have been shaped by masculinity, class, money, and a rather peculiar obsession with suffering. Women, in particular, have historically been denied opportunities, dismissed as less capable, sexualized, patronized, and subjected to all manner of behavior that men seldom have to consider when they show up for a ride.

I've witnessed some of the everyday manifestations myself. Men who suddenly become extraordinarily helpful when a woman shows up in cycling kit. Men whose interest in someone's athletic ability seems suspiciously correlated with how attractive they find her. Men who become uncomfortable when that same woman turns out to be stronger than they are. And men who, upon getting their asses handed to them, retreat to the comforting knowledge that men are generally stronger athletes than women. It's as though belonging to the same sex as a bunch of faster athletes entitles you to borrow accomplishments you haven't earned.

There's nothing inherently wrong with finding another cyclist attractive, offering someone help, or recognizing physiological differences between men and women. The problem is when those considerations begin to matter more than the person standing in front of you. That asymmetry is difficult to ignore. A man can generally show up to a group ride and be treated as another cyclist, practically part of the scenery. A woman, on the other hand, may have to additionally navigate what her presence means to the men around her.

I've been so conscious of that possibility that my own response has often been to avoid interacting much at all. I worried that an attempt at ordinary friendliness might be interpreted as unwanted attention, a compliment might be misconstrued as flirtation, or an otherwise ordinary interaction might inadvertently reproduce the very dynamic I wanted to avoid. So rather than risk behaving differently toward women, I sometimes behaved differently toward women. Brilliant fucking solution.

That apprehension was hardly confined to gender. It extended to people whose backgrounds, experiences, or relationships were different from mine. Sometimes it was simply easier to avoid an interaction than risk making an assumption, saying something insensitive, or unknowingly making someone uncomfortable. The fear of doing something wrong had become a reason to do nothing.

There's a fine line between being considerate of others and being so preoccupied with the possibility of causing harm that you withdraw from the very relationships that might broaden your understanding.

None of this means my concerns were unfounded. Nor does recognizing them establish that I've behaved well. I have almost certainly contributed to some of the problems I criticize, and there are undoubtedly things I still do that I haven't learned to recognize. Awareness isn't innocence, but it isn't guilt, either. And it certainly doesn't imply atonement.

I've come to learn that a stereotype can acquire enough power that you begin modifying your behavior in anticipation of it, even before anyone has actually judged you. Criticism of what we do can be legitimate, specific, and worth taking seriously. Assumptions about who we are based on what we look like or what we enjoy are not automatically entitled to govern our lives.

This made me realize that I don't need to establish my moral credentials before I'm allowed to participate in the world. Growing up half-Japanese and half-white, I've spent much of my life navigating people's assumptions about who I am, what I should be, and where I belong. I've experienced the strange condition of being perceived as an insider and an outsider, sometimes simultaneously, for reasons entirely beyond my control.

In terms of race, I stopped apologizing for who I was a long time ago. I stopped feeling obligated to explain myself to people who had already decided what my identity meant. But somehow, it hadn't occurred to me that I was still doing precisely that when it came to riding my damn bike.

There's an important difference between an ethnicity I didn't choose and a hobby I enthusiastically adopted. I can take off the Lycra. I cannot take off my heritage.

But the instinct to anticipate other people's assumptions and adjust myself accordingly is painfully familiar. To demonstrate that I'm not like the others. To prove that I'm one of the good ones. To explain myself before anyone has even asked.

It's fucking exhausting.

Perhaps more importantly, it doesn't accomplish what I once imagined it would. Being recognized as an exception doesn't necessarily challenge the stereotype. Sometimes it merely reinforces the idea that everyone else deserves it. So I've become less interested in proving that I'm not the stereotypical road cyclist, even though in many respects, I look exactly like him. The more I think about it, the more absurd it seems that I've been carrying that obligation into something I do entirely because it makes me happy.

I enjoy the competition. I enjoy pushing myself to the point of absurdity. I relish the technology, the rituals, the ridiculous equipment, and the satisfaction of beating someone in a sprint by a half a wheel. I'm actually training to race, for fuck's sake. I'm not pretending to be a professional cyclist. I'm a painfully mediocre amateur who happens to take his mediocrity rather seriously and I'm delighted that I can still hold my own against teammates and competitors considerably younger than me. None of that makes me a superior person. It doesn't even necessarily make me a particularly interesting one. But none of it should require an elaborate defense.

The bicycle means something very different to different people. For someone who depends on it for transportation, cycling isn't a hobby requiring thousands of dollars in equipment, a carefully curated Instagram presence, or a weekend group ride that ends with overpriced coffee. For an advocate fighting to make streets safer, the sight of a bunch of recreational road cyclists ignoring a hard-won bike lane might understandably provoke frustration. For someone driving to work, my presence in the roadway might represent an inconvenience they didn't anticipate. I've heard enough horns, threats, slurs, and unsolicited opinions about where I should shove my bicycle to know that some people hate seeing me do what I love.

Transportation, recreation, sport, advocacy, and social connection aren't mutually exclusive pursuits. Sometimes they complement one another and sometimes they conflict. It is entirely possible, though, that same person can participate in all of them before the week is over.

I'm just as happy wandering around the city with friends, bouncing between breweries, hosting a social ride, or introducing someone to a part of town they've never explored. I've organized rides and events because I enjoy creating experiences for other people. I've participated in communities that have given me friendships and relationships I value far more than anything I've accomplished on a bicycle. I commute to work using the very bike lanes I helped advocate. But feeling like I have to list all these achievements as if I'm applying for a license to particpate in my own hobby is complete bullshit. All of these things are true, but I shouldn't need to demonstrate that I've given enough back to the community to justify enjoying something that might otherwise seem frivolous.

Which brings me back to that Reddit comment...

Because underneath the wonderfully vulgar observation about our collective grape smuggling was a criticism I think the cycling community would do well to consider.

There's nothing wrong with taking cycling seriously. Training takes discipline. Racing takes commitment. Organizing a successful community event takes time and effort. Advocating for safer streets takes patience and perseverance. These things can be genuinely difficult, meaningful, and worthy of respect. 

The line gets crossed, however, when some people begin conflating dedication to an activity with authority over everyone else participating in it. They confuse being a strong cyclist with being an important person. They confuse organizing a group ride with owning the community. They confuse sponsorship with legitimacy, visibility with leadership, and criticism with betrayal. The bicycle becomes something more than a bicycle, and not necessarily in a good way.

The irony is that the very activity capable of bringing together people from wildly different backgrounds can become another vehicle for manufacturing status, hierarchy, and exclusion. While I've grown increasingly comfortable with the absurdity of my own preferred form of cycling, I've become considerably less interested in participating in those particular performances. Not because I think I'm above them. Rather, I simply don't want to spend my limited time on this planet turning something I love into another tedious exercise in managing egos.

I don't always get that right. I'm competitive, opinionated, occasionally judgmental, and completely capable of being an asshole. I've changed my mind about people, communities, and my own behavior more than once, and I expect I'll have to do so again.

I just no longer want to preemptively defend myself against every possible interpretation of who I might be. I can recognize the ways cycling excludes people without disavowing the parts of it I love. I can acknowledge my privilege without treating enjoyment as a moral debt. I can be conscious of the harm I might cause without withdrawing from every situation in which that possibility exists. Most importantly, I can be an unapologetic, Lycra-clad, marginal-gain-obsessed competitive cyclist without believing that makes me better than someone riding a rusty department-store bicycle to work.

I'm not asking anyone to recognize me as an exception to the stereotype. I'm increasingly comfortable looking the part. Whether I'm actually behaving like an asshole is another question, and one worth asking on its own merits.

If you think I look like a complete jackass waddling across the sidewalk in my clippy-cloppy shoes, with my weiner hanging out, I probably do. I think it's pretty fucking funny, too. But there's a difference between laughing at what you see and deciding that's all there is to see.

I don't expect everyone to understand why I love this ridiculous sport. I certainly don't always understand why other people love the things they do, either. I have to acknowledge that the stereotypes aren't going anywhere. I could spend the rest of my life trying to prove I'm not *that* cyclist, and there would always be someone convinced otherwise. I can't control that. And frankly, I have no further plans to try to do so.

Every time I let someone else's assumptions make me hesitate to do something I love, I've given those assumptions more authority than they ever deserved.

So I'll keep squeezing into the spandex, waddling around in my ridiculous shoes, and spending entirely too much money and effort trying to make a bicycle go a little faster. Whatever it is that brings you that kind of joy, I hope you do it just as unapologetically.

We should all be so lucky to love something enough to look this fucking ridiculous.
