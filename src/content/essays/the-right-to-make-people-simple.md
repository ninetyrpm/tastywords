### Comparable Things

A while ago, I wrote [this LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7431829624487235584/) about product reviews.

I don't spend much time on LinkedIn, so it was already a suspicious thing to do, but customer feedback was front of mind for me. The team at FirstBuild had just launched a new product and naturally we were closely monitoring the market response. Product reviews are one of the primary sources of signal that we watch, and in most cases, I am pretty good about processing that constructive criticism, but occassionally one of them manages to get under my skin.

Not a catastrophic review. Just an ordinary review. The kind that does exactly what reviews are supposed to do, but somehow still hurts in a stupidly disproportionate way. The worst ones often lack any kind of context, anything that points to a real problem they experienced:

* Three stars. Wouldn’t buy again.

Usually they're only a few sentences long, perhaps with only a single complaint, and often with some backhanded praise offered in the tone of someone leaving a tip on the counter before deciding never to return. Nothing dramatic enough to justify a reaction, which is often exactly the kind of thing that produces one.

A product review takes a lived experience and turns it into something strangers find meaningful. A person buys something with certain expectations about what it does and how it functions. Then they take it home and use it with a particular level of skill, patience, prior experience, and mood. Then the platform asks for all of that to become a star rating.

No one scrolling through product reviews wants an ethnographic study of someone’s kitchen. They want a signal. Did enough people like this thing? Did it break? Are the controls weird? Does it make a weird noise? Can I safely ignore the one person who gave it one star because the delivery driver left it in the rain?

The platform needs comparability, and comparability requires discarding context. This oven can be compared with that oven. This smoker can be compared with that smoker. This sourdough device can be compared with the ancient and honorable tradition of forgetting to feed your starter until it smells like regret.

"Years of work collapse into a star rating," I previously wrote. I initially thought what bothered me was this act of compression, but the more I thought about it, the more I realized that was only the surface explanation. It didn't quite account for why only a few words from a stranger were still bothering me two weeks later.

Physical products arrive in people's lives as if no one made them. A restaurant has a host, a server, and a cook you can imagine even if you never see them. A countertop appliance doesn't arrive that way. If it works, it becomes part of the room. If it fails, the disappointment lands on the object, or the brand, or the vaguely sinister cloud labeled "they."

* They should have made this better.

Behind “they” is usually a tired group of actual people who did, in fact, try. That does not mean the customer is wrong. A product does not become good because good people worked hard on it. The thing either earns its place in someone’s life or it does not. A customer should not have to know the supplier history of a component or the internal debate behind a button before deciding whether the button annoys them.

A product has to work without the footnotes.

### Caring in Advance

Still, the footnotes exist.

The feature that survived three redesigns. The one that did not survive at all. The material that changed because the better one was too expensive, too fragile, too late, or too unavailable. The safety requirement no one will ever thank you for because its success looks exactly like nothing happening.

When you help build products, you learn the shipped thing isn't the dream. Its often merely what survived from the original vision. For a while I thought that was why reviews hurt: years of effort disappearing into a rating, the people disappearing behind the product, and the product getting judged without context as to why it is the way it is. I still think that's true. I just don't think it's the whole reason anymore.

Because the obvious response is: so what? So what if a customer does not imagine the people behind the product? They bought the product, not a relationship with its development team. They do not owe me curiosity. They do not owe anyone gratitude for the meetings. They do not have to sit quietly for an oral history of manufacturability before saying the thing was too loud, too expensive, too confusing, too ugly, or too much trouble to clean.

The maker’s empathy is part of the work. It is not a debt the user agreed to repay. That is what makes the maker-user relationship harsh but mostly honest. To make something for strangers is to care in advance for people who may never care back. The care has to become useful. If it needs to become explanation, something has already gone wrong.

Sitting with this longer, I think my reaction to the review wasn't really about ego or pride in the work. It was a smaller, uglier fear: that the care we painstakingly built into the product hadn't come through to the person using it.

For [Sourdough Sidekick](http://www.sourdoughsidekick.com), the simple version is that the product maintains a sourdough starter. It feeds, monitors, and generally tries to prevent a jar of flour and water from becoming a small countertop accusation of neglect. But the human version is not really about flour and water. It is about the person who wants the ritual of sourdough but keeps failing the maintenance. Someone who likes the idea of tending something alive, but also has a job, a family, a sink full of dishes, and only so much energy to spend on microbial dependents. While the product works just as well for novice and exprienced bakers alike, I personally loved seeing it bring the aspirational bakers to the table.

That is where product work gets interesting to me. Most products aren't profound. Most are just attempts to make some corner of life slightly less stupid. But ordinary life is where most frustration lives, and a good product removes a small burden before the user even has to name it. A bad one becomes another chore wearing the costume of a solution.

So when someone says the product did not work for them, the professional part of you may hear useful feedback. The rational part may hear a data point. The defensive part may even want to explain why they are wrong. But some other part hears something more intimate:

* I was not cared for.

That is obviously not what most reviewers mean. They are tired, disappointed, annoyed, pleased, or simply trying to help the next person avoid a mistake. They are doing exactly what the review system asks them to do: compress.

Still, if the work of making was an attempt to care in advance, then disappointment can feel like evidence that the care wasn't enough. That was the part I could not fully name in the LinkedIn post. And once I'd named it, I started noticing the same operation somewhere else.

### The Machine Turns Around

Once a year, in most corporate systems, the product is no longer the thing being compressed. You are. A year of your work becomes a self-review, an employee rating, or a checkbox in Workday.

* Execution: strong.

* Leadership: developing.

* Strategic thinking: meets expectations.

* Potential: high.

* Areas of opportunity: somehow always present.

A toaster doesn't have rent, health insurance, or a quiet fear of confrontation, so I won't pretend the comparison is exact. But the machinery is familiar enough that I can't unsee it. Product reviews compress experience into stars. Talent reviews compress people into rubrics. In both cases something messy gets trimmed until it is portable. It strips context so that this person can be compared with that person.

The employer might object that it is not evaluating the person, only the performance. However, that distinction gets harder to maintain once the system moves beyond what someone did into what kind of performer they are, what potential they possess, where they belong, and what opportunities should follow. At that point, the abstraction no longer summarizes the work. It begins to govern the worker.

A customer rating a product is participating in an honestly asymmetrical relationship. They bought a thing. They are allowed to be narrow. They are allowed to say, “Two stars. Door feels cheap,” without appending a paragraph acknowledging the complexity of injection molding, hinge suppliers, cost targets, and the spiritual condition of the project team.

The corporation has no such innocence. It isn't a stranger encountering a finished object. It's the structure inside which the work happened. It set the goals, allocated the resources, changed the deadlines, ignored the overload — and then with a straight face and probably a helpful PDF — asked the employee to summarize the year in a few hundred characters.

The user never promised reciprocity. The corporation did.

Maybe not in the contract since contracts are where corporations write down the parts of the relationship they are willing to admit in court. But culturally, rhetorically, and operationally, corporations ask for much more than labor. Ownership. Engagement. Resilience. A growth mindset. A bias for action. It asks employees to experience the organization as a relationship, and then, when it turns back around to evaluate them, experiences them as a resource. It captures the benefits of human care while preserving the right to evaluate that care through systems barely capable of recognizing it.

> The employee is asked to care like a maker. The corporation judges them like a product.

Care doesn't mean the same thing on both sides. In product work, care means anticipating a stranger's needs and building that concern into the object before the stranger ever arrives. In corporate life, care is a posture the institution asks employees to sustain toward all the things that do not fit cleanly inside their job descriptions. The betrayal is not that the corporation fails to return the same kind of care in some sentimental mirror image. The betrayal is that it asks for relationship where value is created, then strips relationship away where power is distributed.

### Collaborating in Your Own Reduction

A few winters ago, I flew across the country and drove through a whiteout blizzard to meet with a partner who was losing confidence in the product we were developing. They were struggling to get the prototype to behave the way it needed to, and the one person on their team who could champion it for us was running out of ammunition. There was no meeting on the calendar that justified the drive. There was merely a relationship potentially eroding in increments too small for anyone to see clearly. I had the sense that if I didn't show up in person, in bad weather, and sit across a table until the specific fear got named and answered, we'd lose their commitment and the weight of their brand.

Nothing about that trip appears in a rubric. It isn't a metric. It isn't even really an accomplishment in the language performance reviews want. There's no line item for "prevented a slow-motion collapse of trust by being willing to drive four hours in a storm." When review season came, I attempted to capture its significance, but a character count made it sound more like a résumé bullet: "managed key partner relationship through proactive engagement."

Anyone who has participated in a formal self-review knows the weirdness of this ritual. You are asked to narrate your own year in the language the institution can process. **You do not simply get compressed. You're recruited to do the compressing yourself.** You take the invisible mentoring, the mistake caught before anyone noticed, the second shift of late-night calls with a manufacturer nine time zones away, and you convert it into competencies and impact statements, because that's the only shape the form can hold.

Naturally, the self-review process is labeled as reflection. Sometimes that is even true. Feedback can be useful, and taking stock of a prior year can be valuable. There are managers who care deeply, teams that handle this thoughtfully, and systems that are less insulting than others. I am not arguing for a workplace governed entirely by vibes, memory, and whoever talks the most in meetings. That way lies its own very special circle of hell.

But inside a formal review system, reflection is never only reflection. It is also translation for administrative use. The process asks you to prove you are more than just a role while speaking entirely in the language of roles.

The polite horror of it is that everyone can be acting in good faith. Your manager may know exactly what the blizzard drive was worth. Your peers may value you. Your team may know what you carried. The person delivering the rating may genuinely understand that the rating is inadequate. They may even say this, with the weary compassion of someone who knows the form is bad but still needs it submitted by Friday. But by the time the process reaches the level where power or authority is distributed — pay, promotion, succession, status, opportunity — care has to pass through the machinery of comparison. 

Employers usually defend their process as fairness, consistency, and objectivity. The implication is that relationship is nice but suspect, while abstraction is serious and neutral. A manager’s direct knowledge is bias. A rubric is objectivity. A conversation is anecdote. A rating is data.

This is a very convenient belief for institutions that have made themselves too large to understand people except through summaries.

### Close Enough to Know

I did not always understand this as clearly as I do now. Earlier in my career, formal review processes felt like one of those unpleasant but apparently inevitable adult rituals, somewhere between tax withholding and pretending to understand health insurance. You filled out the form. You walked a fine line between boasting and humility. You tried to sound honest but not self-sabotaging, confident but not delusional, growth-oriented but not incompetent, ambitious but not threatening, reflective but not in a way that would create extra work for anyone.

I have spent several years working somewhere small enough that this machinery felt distant — not absent, and not perfect, because small teams distort things in their own ways, but close enough that context still had somewhere to live.

The strange thing was that feedback did not become less frequent when the forms receded. It became more immediate and harder to fake. You couldn't spend eleven months being unknowable and then arrive at review season with a tasteful anthology of accomplishments. The work was happening in front of the people who would later have opinions about it, which is both comforting and deeply inconvenient if your preferred leadership style is theatrical invisibility.

But once you've been somewhere that small, it gets harder to accept the destruction of context as inevitable. You start to see the form not as a neutral tool but as a worldview; the perspective that says the important parts of work are the parts that can be summarized for people who weren't there. That compressed language travels faster than the truth and is typically treated as if it were the whole truth. A rating moves through an organization faster and farther than the story behind it. It appears in dashboards, succession plans, comp cycles, and conversations where the person being described isn't even in the room.

Over time the portable version starts to feel more real than the thing it summarized. The restaurant is a 4.2. The product is a 3.7. The employee is a strong performer. The manager is high potential. The team is underperforming. The category survives, not because it's true, but because it is easy to move, easy to repeat, easy to compare, and easy to remember.

### What Gets Removed

Context is not decorative. It is not a sentimental add-on to the real information. Context is the material that tells us what a rating cannot: what someone was asked to solve, what changed around them, what they prevented, what they absorbed, what they made easier for others, and what kind of care their work required. In many cases, their contributions never became visible because their job was to prevent something from breaking.

This is the part the corporate institution has little incentive to examine honestly. Once you admit that the abstraction was created by removing context, you also have to admit that the rating is not neutral. It is not the person in purified form. It is the person after the inconvenient parts have been stripped away. It is a version of them made useful for comparison, allocation, and control.

That is why the problem persists. Not because nobody has noticed that annual reviews are awkward, reductive, demoralizing, and occasionally about as developmentally rich as a dentist asking whether you floss.  It persists because compressed people are easier to compare, rank, reward, deny, move, and explain away. A fully contextual person is harder.

> That difficulty is not a defect. It is the beginning of respect.

So when employers describe these systems as necessary, we should listen carefully for what kind of necessity they mean. Necessary for whom? Necessary for what? Necessary because no better arrangement is imaginable, or because the institution built itself at a scale where human context became administratively inconvenient?

### The Choice Beneath the Ritual

None of this means abstraction is inherently evil. We can't function without it. Maps, averages, rubrics, and shorthand exist because total knowledge isn't available, and if it were, someone would put it in a dashboard and ruin it anyway. The real danger isn't that abstraction exists. It's forgetting that abstraction is an artifact, made by removing the very things from which empathy is built.

I don't think this makes anyone a villain, and I'd be suspicious of an essay that needed one. The manager delivering a rating she knows is inadequate isn't the problem. The employee writing about themselves in the third person isn't the problem. Even the executive who designed the process probably believes, most days, that it's the fairest option available. The dishonesty isn't concentrated in a person. It's distributed across a system that lets everyone inside it act reasonably while the aggregate does something none of them would defend if you asked, in a hallway, without the form in front of them.

That's what makes it a choice rather than an accident: not that someone wants it this way, but that no one has to want it this way for it to keep happening.

> A product may have to work without the footnotes. A person shouldn't have to.

The maker-user asymmetry is harsh but honest — the maker cares for a stranger through an object, and the stranger is free to judge the object without caring back. The employee-corporation asymmetry borrows that honesty without earning it: it asks the employee to care as if inside a relationship, then reserves the right to evaluate them as if outside one.

I can't offer the alternative system that balances fairness and context perfectly, and I'd likely ignore anyone who claims they can. But the absence of a perfect answer isn't a reason to call the current one neutral. That's one of institutional power's oldest tricks — convert a moral problem into an administrative difficulty, then declare it too complicated to challenge.

Call it a betrayal or don't; the word matters less than the habit underneath it. The quiet, repeatable choice to ask for more than a system intends to give back, made easier every cycle by a form that was never going to be asked to answer for it. The remaining question isn't whether a perfect alternative exists. It's how long we keep participating politely in a ritual that makes people smaller for the convenience of an institution that has forgotten it's the one doing the shrinking.

The problem was never that people are too complicated to evaluate cleanly. The problem is that the institution has mistaken its need for cleanliness for a right to make people simple.
