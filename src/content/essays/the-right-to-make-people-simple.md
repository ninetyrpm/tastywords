### Comparable Things

A while ago, I wrote [this LinkedIn post](https://www.linkedin.com/feed/update/urn:li:activity:7431829624487235584/) about product reviews.

This was already a suspicious thing to do. LinkedIn is not where I usually go looking for emotional honesty, unless the emotion has been workshopped into a leadership lesson. But the post was less about announcing something than trying to explain why an ordinary review had gotten under my skin.

Not a catastrophic review. Not a review so unfair it deserved to be printed out and passed around. Just an ordinary review. The kind of review that does exactly what reviews are supposed to do, but somehow still hurts in a stupidly disproportionate way.

* Three stars. Wouldn’t buy again.

A few sentences. Maybe one complaint. Maybe a little praise offered in the tone of someone leaving a tip on a counter before deciding never to return. Nothing dramatic enough to justify a reaction. Which, unfortunately, is often the kind of thing that produces one.

At the time, I thought what bothered me was compression.

A product review takes a lived experience and turns it into something strangers find meaningful. A person buys something, uses it in a particular home, under particular expectations, with a particular level of patience, skill, prior experience, price sensitivity, mood, and tolerance for reading instructions written by someone who lost a fight with legal. Then the platform asks for all of that to become a star rating.

No one scrolling through product reviews wants an ethnographic study of someone’s kitchen. They want a signal. Did enough people like this thing? Did it break? Are the controls weird? Does it make a noise described by multiple reviewers as “concerning”? Can I safely ignore the one person who gave it one star because the delivery driver left it in the rain?

That is what the platform needs: comparability. This oven can be compared with that oven. This smoker can be compared with that smoker. This sourdough device can be compared with the ancient and honorable tradition of forgetting to feed your starter until it smells like regret. The compression is useful because it discards context.

In my original post, I was trying to name the brutality of that compression: “Years of work collapse into a star rating.” That still feels true. Product reviews do flatten nuance. They turn a messy encounter between person, object, expectation, price, patience, and circumstance into something legible enough to compare. But the more I have thought about it, the more I think that was only the surface explanation. It did not quite account for why mere words from a complete stranger were still bugging me.

Physical products arrive in people’s lives as if no one made them. A restaurant still has visible humans attached to it: a host, a server, a bartender, and a cook you can imagine even if you never see them. A countertop appliance does not arrive that way. Neither does a tool, a device, or a piece of hardware. They arrive as things. If they work, they become part of the room. If they fail, the disappointment lands on the object, or the brand, or the vaguely sinister cloud labeled "they."

* They should have made this better.

Behind “they” is usually a tired group of actual people who did, in fact, try. That does not mean the customer is wrong. A product does not become good because good people worked hard on it. Effort is not a warranty. The thing either earns its place in someone’s life or it does not. A customer should not have to know the supplier history of a component or the internal debate behind a button before deciding whether the button annoys them.

A product has to work without the footnotes.

### Caring in Advance

Still, the footnotes exist.

There is always more behind the object than the object can carry: The feature that survived three redesigns. The feature that did not survive at all. The material that changed because the better one was too expensive, too fragile, too late, or too unavailable. The safety requirement no one will ever thank you for because its success looks exactly like nothing happening.

When you help build products, you learn that the shipped thing is not the dream. It is what survived. And for a while, I thought that was why reviews hurt. Years of effort disappeared into a rating. The people disappeared behind the product. Context collapsed so comparison could happen.

I still think all of that is true. I just no longer think it explains why I cared so much.

Because the obvious response is: so what? So what if a customer does not imagine the people behind the product? They bought the product, not a relationship with its development team. They do not owe me curiosity. They do not owe anyone gratitude for the meetings. They do not have to sit quietly for an oral history of manufacturability before saying the thing was too loud, too expensive, too confusing, too ugly, or too much trouble to clean.

The maker’s empathy is part of the work. It is not a debt the user agreed to repay. That is what makes the maker-user asymmetry harsh, but mostly honest. To make something for strangers is to care in advance for people who may never care back. The care has to become useful. If it needs to become explanation, something has already gone wrong.

Product work asks you to imagine the user in great detail. The user is allowed to experience the product as if no maker exists at all. That can feel cold, but it is part of the deal. The relationship is mediated by the thing. If the thing fails, the user is allowed to say so from the only place they actually occupy: their own experience.

The more I sat with this, the more I realized my reaction to reviews was not just about ego, or effort, or pride in the work. It was about the ugly little fear that the care we so painstakingly built into the product did not come through to its users.

For [Sourdough Sidekick](http://www.sourdoughsidekick.com), the simple version is that the product maintains a sourdough starter. It feeds, monitors, and generally tries to prevent a jar of flour and water from becoming a small countertop accusation of neglect.

But the human version is not really about flour and water. It is about the person who wants the ritual of sourdough but keeps failing the maintenance. The person who likes the idea of being someone who tends to something alive, but also has a job, a family, a phone, a sink full of dishes, and only so much energy to spend on microbial dependents. It is about the “oh crap” moment. The tiny shame of forgetting. The gap between the person you meant to be and the Tuesday night you actually had.

That is where product work becomes interesting to me. Not every product is profound, and most are not. Many products are just attempts to make some corner of life slightly less stupid. But ordinary life is where most frustration lives. A good product can remove a small burden before the user has to name it. A bad one can become another chore wearing the costume of a solution.

So when someone says the product did not work for them, the professional part of you may hear useful feedback. The rational part may hear one data point. The defensive part may want to explain why they are wrong. But some other part hears something more intimate:

* I was not cared for.

That is not what most reviewers mean. They are not writing moral philosophy. They are tired, disappointed, annoyed, pleased, or simply trying to help the next person avoid a mistake. They are doing exactly what the review system asks them to do: compress.

Still, if the work of making was an attempt to care in advance, then disappointment can feel like evidence that the care did not make it through. That was the part I could not fully name in the LinkedIn post.

And then, once I had named it, I started noticing the same operation somewhere else.

### The Machine Turns Around

Once a year, in many corporate systems, the product is no longer the thing being compressed.

You are.

A year of work becomes a self-review, a performance rating, a talent category, a compensation recommendation, a development plan, a box in Workday or some other platform named as if it were designed by a committee trying to reassure you that this is all very normal and good for your growth.

* Execution: strong.

* Leadership: developing.

* Strategic thinking: meets expectations.

* Potential: high.

* Areas of opportunity: somehow always present.

I know the comparison sounds too tidy. A product review and a talent review are not the same thing. A toaster does not have rent, health insurance, a professional identity, or a quiet fear of becoming difficult to explain in a meeting.

Still, the machinery was familiar enough that I could not unsee it. Product reviews compress experience into stars. Talent reviews compress people into rubrics. In both cases, something messy gets trimmed until it can travel. Now this person can be compared with that person. But that is where the resemblance stops being enough.

The institution might object that it is not evaluating the person, only the performance. However, that distinction gets harder to maintain once the system moves beyond what someone did into what kind of performer they are, what potential they possess, whether they are ready, where they belong, and what opportunities should follow. At that point, the abstraction no longer summarizes the work. It begins to govern the worker.

A customer rating a product is participating in an honestly asymmetrical relationship. The customer bought a thing. They are allowed to be narrow. They are allowed to say, “Two stars. Door feels cheap,” without appending a paragraph acknowledging the complexity of injection molding, hinge suppliers, cost targets, and the spiritual condition of the project team.

The corporation has no such innocence.

The company is not a stranger encountering a finished object. It is the structure inside which the work happened. It set the goals, allocated the resources, defined the priorities, created the incentives, changed the deadlines, ignored the overload, and then — with a straight face and probably a helpful PDF — asked the employee to summarize the year.

The user did not promise reciprocity. The corporation did.

Maybe not in the contract. Contracts are where corporations write down the parts of the relationship they are willing to admit in court. But culturally, rhetorically, and operationally, corporations ask for much more than labor. They ask for ownership. Engagement. Initiative. Collaboration. Resilience. Adaptability. Passion. Alignment. Customer obsession. A growth mindset. A bias for action. A willingness to go above and beyond, though ideally without creating an overtime problem.

They ask employees to care about the customer, the team, the brand, the mission, the values, the culture, the strategy, the quarter, the next quarter, and the long-term vision, which is sometimes revised before lunch. They ask employees to experience the organization as a relationship. Then, when the institution turns back toward the employee, it often experiences them as a resource.

This is where the asymmetry gets ugly:

> The employee is asked to care like a maker. The corporation judges them like a product.

The comparison to product reviews is useful but not morally equivalent. A product user’s distance is honest because no mutual relationship was promised. The corporation’s distance is dishonest because mutuality is part of the pitch. It captures the benefits of human care while preserving the right to evaluate that care through systems barely capable of recognizing it.

### Collaborating in Your Own Reduction

Anyone who has participated in a formal self-review knows the weirdness of this ritual. You are asked to narrate your own year in the language the institution can process. You take months of work, changed goals, abandoned experiments, invisible mentoring, mistakes recovered before anyone noticed, interpersonal repair, judgment calls, and unglamorous competence, and convert them all into a tidy list of accomplishments, metrics, competencies, growth areas, and impact statements.

You do not simply get compressed. You are asked to help. The self-review is where the institution invites you to collaborate in your own reduction.

Every year, I end up staring at blank website fields that ask me to summarize an entire year’s work in a few hundred characters. Are we studying my performance, or testing my ability to write convincing corporate flash fiction? Sometimes the drawn-out, highly specific things are the story of what you did. The form makes it hard to say, “Hey, remember when I made an emergency trip and drove through a blizzard to meet with a partner and resolve their concerns about our prototype?” There is no obvious place to explain the strange work of specifying a product in carefully chosen technical language, then watching those details pass through another language, another working culture, and another set of assumptions before returning as the thing itself. There is no field for how a weekly hour-long international meeting at 9 p.m. slowly becomes a second shift of Teams calls, WeChat messages, and urgent emails. That was the work, but the form forces me to strip it away.

Naturally, the self-review process is labeled as reflection. Sometimes that is even true. Feedback can be useful. Taking stock of a year can be valuable. There are managers who care deeply, teams that handle this thoughtfully, and systems that are less insulting than others. I am not arguing for a workplace governed entirely by vibes, memory, and whoever talks the most in meetings. That way lies its own very special circle of hell.

But inside a formal review system, reflection is never only reflection. It is also translation for administrative use. The process asks you to prove you are more than just a role while speaking entirely in the language of roles.

The polite horror of it is that everyone can be acting in good faith. Your manager may care about you. Your peers may value you. Your team may know what you carried. The person delivering the rating may genuinely understand that the rating is inadequate. They may even say this, with the weary compassion of someone who knows the form is bad but still needs it submitted by Friday.

And yet the process continues. It carries on this way because the institution has outgrown the scale at which care can remain authoritative.

Care is encouraged right up until it has to compete with the system that actually distributes power. It can soften the conversation. It can inform an anecdote. It can motivate a manager to advocate. But by the time the process reaches the level where power or authority is distributed — pay, promotion, succession, status, opportunity — care has to pass through the machinery of comparison.

Employers usually defend their process as fairness, consistency, calibration, rigor, or maturity. The implication is that relationship is nice but suspect, while abstraction is serious and neutral. A manager’s direct knowledge is bias. A rubric is objectivity. A conversation is anecdote. A rating is data.

This is a very convenient belief for institutions that have made themselves too large to understand people except through summaries. The corporation first creates the conditions under which human context becomes administratively inconvenient, then calls its removal necessary. That is not neutrality. That is architecture pretending to be weather.

Meanwhile, informal feedback systems can be awful in their own ways. They can reproduce favoritism, opacity, old loyalties, personality contests, and the timeless corporate principle that the person most confident in a meeting must be onto something. A small team is not automatically humane because it has fewer forms. Context can protect people, but it can also hide unfairness.

Organizations obviously need to make decisions. Comparison is hard. Abstraction is not automatically justice, though. A standardized process can launder subjective judgment into official language. An annual review meeting can make hierarchy feel like reason. A rating can carry the aura of objectivity precisely because the details that would complicate it have already been removed.

Relationship is not the enemy of fairness. Sometimes relationship is the only place context survives.

### Close Enough to Know

I did not always understand this as clearly as I do now. Earlier in my career, formal review processes felt like one of those unpleasant but apparently inevitable adult rituals, somewhere between tax withholding and pretending to understand health insurance. You filled out the form. You translated yourself into the categories. You tried to sound honest but not self-sabotaging, confident but not delusional, growth-oriented but not incompetent, ambitious but not threatening, reflective but not in a way that would create extra work for anyone.

Then, for several years, I worked in a context where that machinery felt distant. Not absent because everyone had transcended management. Not perfect because small teams create their own distortions. But close enough, or small enough, that context still had somewhere to live.

The strange thing was that feedback did not become less frequent when the forms receded. It became more immediate and harder to fake. You could not spend eleven months being unknowable and then arrive at review season with a tasteful anthology of accomplishments. The work was happening in front of the people who would later have opinions about it, which is both comforting and deeply inconvenient if your preferred leadership style is theatrical invisibility.

I do not want to romanticize informality. It has risks. Uneven standards can hide inside flexibility. Bias can hide inside familiarity. A small team can confuse being known with being understood. Still, once you have worked somewhere small enough for context to survive, it becomes harder to accept its destruction as inevitable. You begin to see the form not merely as a tool, but as a worldview.

It says the most important parts of work are the parts that can be summarized for people who were not there. It says fairness means making unlike things comparable. It says relationship is valuable until it becomes too local to audit. It says your year counts most when it can be converted into language that travels, and compressed language travels better than people do. That is what makes it powerful.

A star rating can move through a marketplace faster than the full story of a product experience. A performance rating can move through an organization faster than the full story of the work. It can appear in dashboards, compensation cycles, succession plans, leadership updates, talent discussions, and conversations where the person being described is not present. The abstraction becomes portable in a way the reality cannot.

Over time, the portable version starts to feel more real than the thing it summarized. The restaurant is a 4.2. The product is a 3.7. The employee is a strong performer. The manager is high potential. The team is underperforming. The category survives because it is easy to move, easy to repeat, easy to compare, and easy to remember.

The danger is not that abstraction exists. We cannot live without abstraction. We need maps, categories, names, averages, ratings, rubrics, rules of thumb, and shorthand. We need ways to act without total knowledge because total knowledge is not available, and even if it were, someone would eventually put it in a dashboard and ruin it.

The real danger is forgetting that the abstraction is an artifact. It was made by removing things. And often the things removed are exactly the things from which empathy is built.

### What Gets Removed

Context is not decorative. It is not a sentimental add-on to the real information. Context is the material that tells us what a rating cannot: what someone was carrying, what they were asked to solve, what changed around them, what they prevented, what they absorbed, what they made easier for others, and what kind of care their work required. In many cases, their contributions never became visible because their job was to prevent something from breaking. As my father often said, "a thousand 'atta-boys' can be wiped out by a single 'aw shit'".

This is the part the institution has little incentive to examine honestly. Once you admit that the abstraction was created by removing context, you also have to admit that the rating is not neutral. It is not the person in purified form. It is the person after the inconvenient parts have been stripped away. It is a version of them made useful for comparison, allocation, and control.

I do not know how large organizations should make every decision. I am not pretending context scales cleanly, or that relationship is always fairer than process. But I know the current ritual asks us to treat the loss of context as a sign of seriousness, and that is where I stop believing it.

That is why the problem persists. Not because nobody has noticed that annual reviews are awkward, reductive, demoralizing, and occasionally about as developmentally rich as a dentist asking whether you floss. Everyone knows. The jokes are too easy because the ritual is too familiar.

The ritual does not continue because it is beloved. It persists because compressed people are easier to compare, rank, reward, deny, move, and explain away. A fully contextual person is harder. That difficulty is not a defect. It is the beginning of respect.

So when corporations describe these systems as necessary, we should listen carefully for what kind of necessity they mean. Necessary for whom? Necessary for what? Necessary because no better arrangement is imaginable, or necessary because the institution has designed itself around forms of distance that now require human beings to be simplified?

> Not having a perfect solution is not an excuse for accepting the status quo.

There may be no universal system that perfectly balances efficiency and empathy, comparison and context, fairness and relationship. Fine. Human beings are difficult. Work is complicated. Organizations are messy. No form will save us. No manager will see everything. No anti-rubric utopia is waiting patiently behind the next offsite.

But the absence of a silver bullet does not make the current machinery innocent. That is one of institutional power’s oldest tricks: convert a moral problem into an administrative difficulty, then declare it too complicated to challenge.

I make no claim that every review, rating, rubric, or abstraction could be abolished tomorrow. My argument is that we should stop allowing these systems to present themselves as neutral. We should stop letting them describe the destruction of context as fairness. We should stop accepting the idea that relationship is bias when abstraction is often just bias with better formatting.

### The Choice Beneath the Ritual

A corporation that asks people to care deeply, then insists on understanding them shallowly, is making a choice. A corporation that depends on relationship where work happens, then strips relationship from the places where power is distributed, is making a choice. A corporation that asks employees to compress themselves into forms, goals, ratings, and talent review language, and then treats the result as objective, is making a choice. The fact that the choice is normal does not make it less grotesque.

A product may have to work without the footnotes. A person should not have to.

The asymmetry between maker and user may be harsh, but it is honest. The maker chooses to care for a stranger through an object, and the stranger is free to judge the object without caring back.

The asymmetry between employee and corporation is different. The corporation asks the employee to care as if they are in a relationship, then evaluates them as if they are an object.

That cannot be softened into a tradeoff. It should be named as a betrayal. And once it is named, the remaining question is not whether the perfect alternative exists. The remaining question is how long we are willing to keep participating politely in rituals that make people smaller for the convenience of institutions that have forgotten they are the ones doing the shrinking.

The problem is not that people are too complicated to evaluate cleanly.

The problem is that the institution has mistaken its need for cleanliness for a right to make people simple.
