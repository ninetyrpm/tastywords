### The Shortcut

Suppose I discover a better way across town.

Not a new road. I do not pour asphalt or petition the city for a bridge. I simply spend enough time moving through the existing streets to notice something other people have missed: a particular sequence of alleys, side streets, cut-throughs, and well-timed traffic lights that turns a forty-minute trip into thirty-two.

It is not obvious. The route looks worse on a map. One of the turns feels counterintuitive. But after enough experimenting, I find it. 
I tell a few friends. They try it. It works.

Maybe I publish the route online. Maybe I become known, to the extremely limited extent anyone can become known for such a thing, as the person who figured out the thirty-two-minute way across town. I would probably enjoy that.

Now suppose somebody else starts using it. I would not consider that theft. I still have the route. They have not deprived me of anything. If anything, I might be pleased to see someone else benefiting from something I discovered.

Suppose they repost my exact map without attribution and claim they found the route themselves. That feels pretty bad.

Suppose they package my map, my written directions, my photographs, and my clever little name for the route and begin selling copies. That feels even worse.

Suppose they never saw my map at all. They live across town, encounter the same traffic patterns, make the same strange turn behind the grocery store, and independently arrive at almost exactly the same route. In that case, I would have a difficult time explaining why they had done anything wrong.

Now imagine the city steps in. I formally claim the route, and the city grants me an exclusive right to it for roughly the next twenty years. Other people may still travel across town, of course. They just cannot follow the particular sequence of streets I claimed. The other person who independently discovered the same route must stop using it.

A delivery company that wants to use it can pay me. Or they can instead attempt to devise a sufficiently different route around mine. Perhaps they take the same streets but turn one block earlier, add a technically unnecessary hill, and then double back through a parking lot. Teams of highly educated people then spend weeks determining how close one route can come to another before somebody should call a lawyer.

This whole situation would be ridiculous, right?

I participate in a version of this system for a living.

### Inventor

My first patent is not exciting. It is titled:

* US 8,907,541: *Slot Liner for Electro-Dynamic Machine*.

Even the title seems determined to prevent curiosity.

To understand why I was proud of it requires a small amount of knowledge about automotive alternators, which is already asking a lot from anyone who has managed to avoid developing opinions about automotive alternators.

An alternator contains a stator: roughly speaking, a laminated steel structure packed with copper windings. The copper needs to be electrically insulated from the steel around it, so insulating material is inserted into the slots before the windings go in. In the particular manufacturing world I had entered after college, that insulation was a paper-like material called Nomex.

Every bit of insulating material occupying the slot is space that cannot be occupied by copper. Engineers care intensely about how much copper can be crammed into those slots because more copper, arranged correctly, can mean better electrical performance. The ratio has a name—slot fill—and like many engineering terms it sounds deeply uninteresting right up until somebody tells you that fractions of a millimeter are now standing between you and an efficiency target.

I did not arrive at my first job understanding any of this. It took years.

I spent time in factories learning how stators were produced. I visited tooling and equipment vendors in other states. I traveled to manufacturing facilities in other countries. I sat through meetings about quality, throughput, cost, winding processes, insulation, noise, eddy currents, and all the other details that accumulate until a machine stops looking like a machine and starts looking like several hundred small compromises stacked on top of one another.

The insulating paper had always seemed like a necessary boundary between the copper and steel. But I also knew how the liners themselves were manufactured and inserted. Somewhere in the accumulation of all that specialized, deeply niche knowledge was another way of doing it: an idea  that eventually became a patent.

The company gave inventors plaques when patents issued. Mine was beautiful: wood with a black acrylic overlay, with the invention laser engraved into it. I put it proudly on display at my desk. Years later, when I left that company, I took it with me and displayed it at another one.

The plaque didn't age well, but my pride in what it represented endured. Not because I thought the world had been waiting impatiently for an improved slot liner. I was proud because the plaque embodied something the title could not. It represented years spent becoming competent enough in a difficult field to see something differently. It meant I had crossed some invisible threshold from the young engineer trying to understand why everyone did things the way they did to an engineer capable of saying: perhaps we don't have to.

It was not just recognition of the idea. It felt like recognition of the person I had become capable of having it.

There was also money.

The company paid an incentive when an invention disclosure was accepted and another when the patent ultimately issued. I was young enough, and not especially far removed from college, that a check was an excellent philosophical tranquilizer.

The patent had my name on it.

**Inventor.**

It also had the company's name.

**Assignee.**

I noticed the distinction. I cannot claim that I immediately recognized it as some profound ideological contradiction. Mostly I was thrilled to have a patent. But the second line took a little something away from the first.

I had invented it. They owned it.

### Assignee

There is a perfectly reasonable explanation for this.

The company paid my salary. It employed me specifically to solve engineering problems. It paid for the laboratories and factories, the equipment, the prototypes, the travel, the vendors, the legal work, and much of the education that made the invention possible in the first place. I was not tinkering alone in a garage and having a multinational corporation arrive afterward to claim my thoughts.

I had signed an employment agreement. My inventions belonged to the company.

This arrangement is generally described as voluntary, which is true in the same technical sense that I was free to pursue a career in something other than corporate engineering. I do not remember anyone sliding two employment contracts across the table, one in which the company owned inventions developed through my work and another in which I retained them, perhaps with different salary options printed neatly underneath.

“No, thank you. I would prefer to retain exclusive ownership of whatever I invent using your salary, laboratories, equipment, suppliers, and development budget” is not a negotiating position with broad appeal in industrial R&D.

Still, I understood the bargain and had no compelling case at the time to challenge it. What complicated my views was learning what ownership looked like from the other side.

At that same company, we began using plasma welding to connect hairpin conductors in electric-machine stators. Hairpin windings were a relatively new manufacturing advantage over the more traditional randomly wound designs, and plasma welding offered technical and manufacturing benefits over some of the resistance-welding methods we had used previously. We used it frequently in our low-volume and prototype manufacturing plant.

Then, while developing a particular product, we encountered a competitor's patent. I no longer remember the claims well enough to describe them precisely, and I am sure the actual patent was considerably more sophisticated than my memory of it. What I remember is the shape.

Plasma welding the conductors naturally produced something like a ball at the joint. The competitor's patent apparently claimed some aspect of that geometry and a technical advantage associated with it—perhaps cooling, perhaps something else that has been overwritten by several careers' worth of newer engineering trivia. What survived perfectly intact was the reaction.

The senior engineers thought it was fucking dumb. I distinctly remember one of them complaining, "They patented that water is wet and the sun is hot." That phrase lodged itself permanently in my brain.

It was not a legal analysis. The legal team did the legal analysis, spending hours evaluating the claims and the potential infringement risk and offering variations of the answer engineers eventually learn to expect from attorneys:

* It depends.

But the engineering reaction was simpler. Plasma welding naturally makes this shape. Someone had been granted a right around that shape. Therefore we needed another shape.

I do not remember every step of the ultimate solution. I do remember a press. We would make the plasma weld in essentially the normal way. Then a press would come down afterward and simply put a dimple in the ball.

There were technical advantages claimed for the dimple, perhaps genuine ones. Memory is especially unreliable when reconstructing technical decisions made many years ago, and I do not want to reduce a complicated patent to a punch line simply because the punch line was memorable. But even now, some part of me remains convinced that the most important property of that dimple was legal.

* Someone owned round. We made it not-round.

At the time this felt more absurd than philosophically disturbing. Engineering contains endless constraints: cost, materials, tooling, packaging space, regulations, temperatures, vibration, customers, suppliers. You solve around them. Patent claims merely became another wall in the maze.

But unlike the others, this wall had not been put there by physics. We had built it ourselves.

### It Depends

A few years later, I learned how large the maze could become.

Automotive start-stop systems were becoming increasingly common. Stop at a traffic light and the engine shuts off. Remove your foot from the brake or press the accelerator and the engine starts again. 
The delay is brief, but brief is not the same thing as imperceptible.

One of our customers wanted what was known as a change-of-mind starter. If the driver changed their mind while the engine was still spinning down, the starter would not wait for everything to come to a complete stop. Instead, the starter motor would accelerate the pinion to synchronize with the still-decelerating engine before engaging the ring gear and cranking it again. The advantage was measured in fractions of a second. Fractions of a second matter surprisingly much when the light turns green and your foot has already moved to the gas pedal.

There was a mechanical challenge. The ring gear carried substantial momentum, and a poorly managed engagement could send severe back-torque through the starter components. A slip clutch could protect the mechanism. Unfortunately, another company had already spent time in this territory. Their solution involved a Belleville-washer-type clutch arrangement, not unlike what our engineers had already developed and tested. Their patent sat directly in the landscape we needed to cross. A special team was assembled to find another way.

For perhaps six or eight weeks, we held dedicated workshops around this problem. I learned how to read patents more seriously. I learned that understanding an invention and understanding what a patent claims about that invention are not the same skill. I learned to appreciate the almost comic mismatch between the precision engineers want and the conditional language lawyers are trained to provide.

* Can we do this?

* It depends.

* What if we change this?

* It depends.

* Would this infringe?

* Maybe.

Eventually we developed another approach. My memory of the final mechanism has faded, although I remember tolerance rings and a surprising amount of statistical analysis. What has not faded is the amount of engineering effort directed specifically toward finding a path through the existing intellectual-property landscape.

We succeeded. The company retained a contract worth tens of millions of dollars. Ultimately, the CEO handed out awards for the team's contributions, myself included. Next to my plaque, I added a miniature model of a Spitfire plane. I became quite proud of that, too. Which is where the story becomes inconvenient.

It would be easy now to describe those six or eight weeks as pure waste: talented engineers diverted from useful work because a competitor had fenced off an obvious solution.

But we did useful engineering. We learned things. We developed another mechanism. It may even have been better in some respects. The patent constrained us, and constraint can produce creativity. Every engineer has experienced that.

So did the patent promote innovation? Or did it force a room full of engineers to spend two months innovating around an artificial restriction? I don't know.

What I do know is that by relatively early in my career I had been rewarded on both sides of the same institution. I got a plaque because my company had secured a patent around something I invented. I got an award because I helped my company escape a patent around something somebody else invented.

Both were considered innovation.

### What Does “Protect” Mean?

The strongest defense of patents begins somewhere I agree with completely.

> Creators deserve protection.

The sentence sounds so obviously correct that almost nobody bothers asking what the word *protection* contains. Protection from what? Suppose I spend years developing something useful. What should follow from that?

Should I receive credit for having created it? I think so.

Should I be compensated for my work? Hopefully.

If I spent substantial money developing it, should I have some realistic opportunity to recover that investment? That seems important.

Should somebody be able to reproduce my exact implementation, erase my name, attach theirs, and pretend the work was theirs all along? Absolutely not.

Should I receive some commercial advantage for taking the risk first? Perhaps.

Should I—or the corporation to which I assigned the invention—be able to prevent another person from independently arriving at the same underlying solution and practicing it with materials they own?
That is where my intuition starts resisting.

These questions are usually bundled into a single concept: intellectual-property protection. Question that bundle and another equally compressed concept appears almost immediately: Theft.

Either we protect inventors or we let everyone steal their work. This is a remarkably effective argument because one of its options has already been named stealing. But there is not one knob labeled **INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY**, with **FREE-FOR-ALL** on the left and **CIVILIZATION** on the right.

There is a control panel.

Attribution is one control. Compensation is another. Exclusivity is another. Duration, scope, commercial use, independent invention, transferability, licensing, disclosure, and whether anyone actually intends to practice the invention are all separate controls. Turn one without turning another and you get a different system.

We could require attribution without exclusivity. We could distinguish copying from independent discovery. We could make rights narrower or shorter. We could treat pharmaceuticals differently from mechanical linkages, novels differently from software, individuals differently from enormous corporate portfolios. We could require inventions to be practiced or licensed. We could use prizes, compulsory licensing, public funding, first-mover advantage, trade secrets, open standards, or arrangements nobody has yet bothered to imagine.

Some of those alternatives would undoubtedly create their own terrible incentives. The point is not that I know where every knob should be set. The point is that the knobs exist.

### Inventing Invention

Patents changed meaning for me as I accumulated more of them. The first was an achievement. Later, they became part of the job.

Some employers formalize that progression with milestone rewards as the count rises: medals, glass plaques, trophies, additional financial incentives. Eventually something that once felt like proof that you had crossed a professional threshold acquires a punch card. The achievement becomes a metric.

Work on a new product with any serious chance of commercialization and, sooner or later, somebody asks the question:

* Anything patentable?

That question makes sense. I currently work at FirstBuild, a place explicitly organized around finding latent or unmet needs and developing new solutions around them. The underlying assumption is that I invent things.

But *invent* has at least two meanings in this environment.

There is inventing in the ordinary sense: understanding a problem deeply enough to create something novel and useful. Then there is inventing in the institutional sense: identifying something that might support an intellectual-property claim.

Those activities overlap. They are not the same.

I have seen engineers who are extraordinarily effective at producing patentable concepts without much concern for whether anyone will ever build them. The incentive structure rewards this. Departments can have IP targets. Leaders want portfolios around markets they care about. Sometimes they want portfolios around markets they do not currently care about, because someday they might.

Even concepts with uncertain commercial futures may be worth capturing. If we decide not to use them, the reasoning sometimes seems to become: perhaps someone else shouldn't be able to use them either.

The incentive is understandable from the company's perspective. A patent is not merely a commemorative document celebrating an engineer's cleverness. It is an asset. It can defend a product, strengthen a negotiating position, increase a portfolio's value, discourage a competitor, create licensing opportunities, or occupy territory around a future business. The strange part is how different that feels from the inventor's side.

For an engineer, a patent can represent years of accumulated experience: the factories visited, the prototypes that failed, the machine taken apart at midnight, the weird detail nobody understood until someone finally did. There is blood, sweat, frustration, creativity, and occasionally the satisfaction of seeing a problem surrender.

For the organization, all of that eventually has to become legible. An inventor's toil is reduced to: Novelty. Claim scope. Commercial importance. Competitive value. Filing cost. Portfolio count.

The personal significance and the commercial significance are not mutually exclusive. They simply aren't the same thing.

### Lost in Translation

There is yet another strange transformation along the way.

> I often cannot understand my own patents.

I can still explain my very first patent relatively easily. In fact, I just did earlier in this essay. But let's take a look at its first claim:

* A slot liner configured for association with a tooth of an electro-dynamic machine core having an annular rim and circumferentially spaced teeth radially extending from the annular rim, the slot liner comprising: sidewalls configured to line circumferential surfaces of the tooth; first and second tabs formed by bends in the sidewalls, the first tab being configured to line a portion of the annular rim adjacent to the tooth and the second tab being configured to overlap with the first tab of an adjacent slot liner; and a retainer configured to retain the sidewalls against a radially inward portion of the tooth.

Apparently, I invented that.

The funny thing is that I understand every one of those words. I can explain the underlying invention to another engineer using the language we actually use, and I can usually explain it to someone outside the field with enough analogies and hand waving.

But somewhere between my explanation and the issued patent, the idea has been translated into another dialect. Patent agents and attorneys generalize terms, anticipate alternatives, respond to examiners, account for prosecution history, and construct claims intended to survive a future argument I may never witness. 

The result is legally meaningful, but it can also feel oddly alien.

Occasionally I read something bearing my name and have the peculiar experience of wondering what, exactly, I patented. I am the inventor, but the thing describing my invention has become partially illegible to me.

Perhaps this is inevitable. Specialized institutions develop specialized language. But it reinforces the quiet distinction I first noticed on that plaque.

**Inventor.**

**Assignee.**

One describes where the idea came from. 

The other describes what the system turned it into.

### The Architecture We Forgot We Built

I spend a great deal of my professional life separating requirements from solutions. This sounds almost insultingly obvious until you watch how often intelligent people confuse them.

Someone says, “We need a button here.” Usually we do not.

We need the user to initiate an action. A button is one possible solution.

That distinction matters because the moment the button becomes a requirement, an entire design space disappears. We stop asking whether there is a more intuitive interaction because someone has accidentally smuggled an implementation into the specification.

I increasingly wonder whether we have done the same thing with intellectual property. The requirements might be that we want useful things to be created. We want people and organizations to take expensive risks in pursuit of them. We want inventors to receive credit. We want companies to have reason to disclose discoveries rather than keep everything secret. We want small creators to have some protection against larger organizations that can copy their work faster than they can commercialize it.

We want knowledge to spread. We want subsequent innovation to remain possible. These are real and sometimes conflicting requirements.

A patent is a solution architecture. It gives a qualifying invention a temporary, transferable, and state-enforced right to exclude others from practicing what is claimed. Maybe that is a good architecture. Maybe in some industries it is the least bad architecture anyone has found. But those are conclusions that should have to survive an argument.

Instead, the architecture often presents itself as the requirement. Innovation requires patents. Creators require intellectual property. Without ownership there is theft.

These assumptions are embedded so deeply into the environments in which I have spent my career that questioning them can feel unserious and, in some corporate settings, borderline heretical.

Intellectual property is not peripheral to this kind of work. Producing it can be part of how engineers, teams, and entire departments are evaluated. Questioning its underlying legitimacy can therefore sound less like philosophical curiosity than doubt about whether you are willing to perform part of the job you were hired to do.

I am sometimes reminded of the familiar response to criticism of capitalism: how can you object to capitalism while owning a smartphone?

The argument treats participation as consent. By that standard I am in particularly poor standing to question patents. I have them. I have received money for them. I have displayed one proudly on my desk. I work for organizations that benefit from them. Capturing patentable inventions is literally part of what I am paid to do.

None of that places me external to the contradiction. It places me directly inside it. I benefit from a system I distrust. That does not prove the system is wrong. It also does not prove my doubts disappear when the bonus clears.

Perhaps some of my discomfort comes from a broader suspicion of categories that become so familiar we stop noticing they are categories at all.

Constructed does not mean illegitimate. Currency is constructed. Corporations are constructed. Contracts are constructed. Property lines are constructed.  Useful things are designed all the time. But successful designs have a strange tendency to disappear into the background. After enough generations, we stop perceiving them as designs. They become simply how things are.

That is when I become suspicious. Because one of the first questions an experienced engineer asks when inheriting an old product is whether the thing everyone calls a requirement is actually just something somebody decided twenty years ago.

### I Don't Know the Better Way

This is usually the part where criticism becomes obligated to produce a replacement.

Bottom line: I don't have one.

I have read arguments for eliminating intellectual property almost entirely. I have read the conventional arguments for protecting it. I find parts of both persuasive and parts of both too neat.

A pharmaceutical company spending billions of dollars bringing a drug through development is not solving the same economic problem as an engineer designing a bracket. A novel is not a plasma weld. Software is not a washing machine. A lone inventor facing a multinational corporation is not situated like one multinational corporation facing another.

Perhaps the greatest mistake would be recognizing one false binary and replacing it with another. Patents or no patents. Property or commons. Capitalism or whatever word causes the conversation to immediately become unbearable at Thanksgiving.

I am much more interested in reopening the design space. Maybe attribution and exclusivity do not have to travel together. Maybe independent invention should matter. Maybe rights should differ dramatically by domain. Maybe some kinds of development genuinely require a temporary monopoly while others are slowed by it. Maybe the best mechanisms would look less like property and more like licensing, prizes, compulsory access, first-mover advantage, or things we have not designed yet.

Or maybe, after all, the current system is better than the alternatives. But if it is, I want it to win that argument rather than inherit it.

That feels like an embarrassingly modest conclusion after a career spent helping create the things I am questioning. 

Tomorrow, or next week, I will probably return to a project review. We will be talking about some new product, some unmet need, some prototype that finally works. Someone will ask the familiar question.

* Anything patentable?

I will take the question seriously. I will probably identify something.

We may document it. An agent may translate it. Claims may be written around it. The company may decide the idea is worth the filing fees and the years of prosecution that follow. Perhaps, eventually, another plaque will exist.

My name will appear beside **Inventor**.

Another name will appear beside **Assignee**.

And somewhere in the future, another engineer may encounter the resulting patent while trying to solve a problem of their own. Maybe they will license it. Maybe they will discover something better. Maybe they will spend six weeks finding a route around it.

I still do not know exactly what the right system should look like. But I am increasingly certain of only one thing:

> The system we have is not a law of nature.

It is another invention. We should be willing to treat it like one.
