Nearly eight years ago, I was leading the development of an over-the-range microwave at GE Appliances. It would be the company’s first microwave of its kind with Wi-Fi connectivity. At the time, the company was pursuing a broader strategy to connect every appliance it sold. The organizational value of a connected appliance ecosystem—and the data it could produce—was easy enough to understand. The value to the person standing in front of any particular appliance was sometimes less obvious. For some products, the connection was fairly direct. For a microwave, we had to work a little harder to find it.

This prompted a question from more than a few people on the team, including me:

* Why the hell does a microwave need to connect to the internet?

The value of connectivity to the company was easy enough to understand. A large installed base of connected appliances could enable diagnostics, software updates, coordination across products, and, perhaps most importantly, a much richer understanding of how those products were actually being used. But all of that depended on the person who bought the microwave seeing enough value to connect it in the first place. That was the harder question. The most obvious consumer-facing benefit—remote operation—became strange when applied to a box whose primary function required physically putting food inside it and returning a few minutes later to take the food back out.

At the same time, I was part of a smaller group that met occasionally to discuss innovation in the microwave and ventilation space. This was not an easy category in which to feel innovative. Microwaves were mature, heavily commoditized products competing for market share through fairly incremental differences: another sensor, another cooking mode, another control scheme, another cosmetic variation, another feature that could be printed on the carton.

At one point, partly as provocation, I proposed a microwave with one button.

Press it: thirty seconds.

Press it again: thirty more.

I was not seriously proposing that every microwave abandon power levels, defrosting, timers, and every other function accumulated over decades. What bothered me was the assumption hiding beneath the opposite approach. Put two otherwise comparable microwaves next to each other, one with nine cooking modes and one with eighteen, and eighteen sounds like more appliance. Capability is countable. Countable differences are easy to merchandise.

But if quantity of functions were really the compelling value proposition, microwaves should have devolved into an arms race of buttons decades ago. We would be selling control panels with microwave ovens attached.

So I turned to the internet to look for anything that might help answer the question I was ostensibly being paid to answer: what could make the next microwave meaningfully different from the last one? I do not remember making a deliberate decision to start reading academic papers.

Part of it was probably laziness. It was easier to sit at my desk and search the web than to do the much harder work of getting close enough to customers to understand what was actually frustrating them, what they were improvising around, or what they wished the product did differently. I know how to approach that work now. At the time, I really didn’t.

I also was not working in an environment particularly organized around that kind of discovery. We knew how to develop appliances. We knew how to benchmark competitors, improve specifications, add features, hit cost targets, and move products through a development process. What we were less practiced at was starting with someone’s lived experience and turning an unmet need into a product opportunity.

The internet was an easier place to look for answers. But companies were not exactly publishing the thinking behind their innovation strategies. Most of what surfaced was the finished product: press releases, feature lists, concept videos, and polished explanations of whatever had already launched. More substantial material was often attached to a consultancy or research service with something to sell. Academia, oddly, was more accessible.

Google Scholar, ResearchGate, Academia.edu, open-access journals, and the occasional paper that escaped a publisher’s paywall offered something different. The articles were rarely answering the questions I was actually asking. They did not tell me what the next microwave feature should be, how to make a smart appliance compelling, or how to run a better new-product-development process. But they were at least trying to explain why people used technologies the way they did, how domestic practices changed, what convenience meant, and what assumptions designers carried into the home.

Somewhere along the way, I accidentally wandered into sociology and anthropology.

One of the papers I found was Genevieve Bell and Joseph Kaye’s *Designing Technology for Domestic Spaces: A Kitchen Manifesto*. They traced the long history of applying industrial ideas about efficiency to domestic life, including the migration of Taylorism from factories into the kitchen. Their argument was that technologists had repeatedly treated the home as a collection of tasks waiting to be optimized while paying too little attention to the fact that people actually live there. Kitchens produce meaning as well as meals. Their manifesto eventually distilled the provocation into four words: **“Value experience over efficiency.”** 

I thought this was academic, philosophical drivel.

It did not tell me why a microwave needed Wi-Fi. It did not tell me what features we should develop. It did not produce an innovation roadmap, a technical requirement, a competitive advantage, or anything else I could take into a program review. So I moved on.

But some of it stuck.

Bell and Kaye were not really arguing against efficiency. They were asking why efficiency had become so naturalized as a virtue that designers rarely had to defend it as an objective at all. Faster, easier, more automatic, more capable: these things simply arrived pre-labeled as improvements. The question of what experience all that efficiency was supposed to support came later, if it came at all. 

Another paper gave me two words that have stayed lodged in my head much longer than I remembered their source.

In 2003, Antonella Carù and Bernard Cova were criticizing a different trend in marketing: the growing insistence that consumption ought to be extraordinary, immersive, memorable, intense. Their concern was that in trying to fill life with ever more experience, we were losing something more ordinary and less marketable: contemplative time.

They described a **“dual trend of saturation and the acceleration of time.”** Saturation was the tendency to fill available moments with activity. Acceleration was the tendency to perform those activities ever more quickly so that more could fit. The result was not merely busyness in the sense of having a lot to do. It was a way of organizing time around the pressure to extract more experience from it. 

The terms saturation and acceleration embedded in my brain some reason. Perhaps because they sounded almost mechanical, they gave engineering language to something unmistakably human.

Carù and Cova were interested in what this did to experience. If every moment had to contain something, and every experience had to be intensified into something memorable, then ordinary time began to look like wasted time. Their alternative leaned toward what they called “space”: experiences that gave time back rather than consuming it, that allowed contemplation instead of demanding constant intensity. I was less interested in their proposed escape than in the mechanism they had named.

Around the same period, other reading made that mechanism harder to ignore. Judy Wajcman, drawing on sociologist Hartmut Rosa, describes a paradox at the center of technological acceleration. If technology reduces the time required for production, transportation, communication, and ordinary tasks, then technological progress ought to produce more free time. Instead, technological acceleration and a growing scarcity of time seem perfectly capable of increasing together. 

The point is not simply that technology makes us busy. New technologies change what is possible, and practices reorganize around those possibilities. Faster communication creates more communication. Easier access creates more things worth accessing. The time saved from one activity does not remain in a protected account marked *leisure*. The surrounding system changes.

Then I encountered Yolande Strengers and Larissa Nicholls writing about convenience in smart homes. Their argument brought the whole thing uncomfortably close to my actual industry. Technologies marketed as labor-saving can genuinely make individual practices easier while failing to reduce total household labor or energy use. Convenience can change expectations and practices around the technology itself. Smart-home systems can add devices and complexity that then require still more automation and integration to make that complexity manageable. 

Somewhere among those readings, the ideas fused in my head.

Bell and Kaye had made me suspicious of efficiency as an unquestioned objective. Carù and Cova had given me saturation and acceleration. Wajcman and Rosa had given me the paradox of time saved without time gained. Strengers and Nicholls had shown how convenience could alter the practice it was supposedly simplifying.

I started seeing a loop. And for some reason, the example I kept returning to was laundry.

### Laundry

The modern washing machine is an extraordinarily successful labor-saving device. A task that once demanded substantial physical work can now require little more than loading a drum, adding detergent, selecting a cycle, and walking away. Dryers continue the trick. The machine does not merely perform the work faster; it allows much of the work to happen without us.

The usual story of convenience is straightforward. A burden existed. Technology reduced it. The difference became ours. Except I kept wondering what happened to the difference.

Imagine, simplistically, that maintaining a small wardrobe once required enough human effort that repeatedly wearing and washing a small number of garments made sense. Mechanization changes the equation. I can put a week’s worth of clothing into a machine and clean all of it with relatively little additional effort. So I can own more clothing.

Obviously, the washing machine did not cause the modern wardrobe. Cheap manufacturing, fast fashion, changing standards of dress, rising consumption, larger homes, and plenty of other economic and social forces have much more to say about that. Still, the machine changes what is practical.

A larger wardrobe is easier to maintain when maintenance is cheap. More clothes can accumulate between washes because the machine can process them together. A large hamper makes sense because laundry happens in batches. Eventually a household can comfortably sustain a volume of clothing that would have created an absurd amount of labor under an earlier system.

Then laundry becomes a burden again. The hamper fills. Loads accumulate. We set aside a "laundry day". We shop for larger drums, shorter cycles, automatic detergent dispensing, better sensing, quieter operation, specialized fabric care, remote notifications, or any of the other ways an appliance can absorb more demand with less intervention.

At first, this still looked like Carù and Cova’s saturation and acceleration. The laundry system fills until its fullness becomes consequential. We respond by finding ways to get through the burden faster or with less effort. The washing machine is the acceleration.

But something in that description never quite fit.

A larger drum is not faster. A load sensor is not faster. Automatic detergent dosing is not faster. Wi-Fi certainly does not make microwave radiation travel faster. What these interventions can do is increase the range or amount of demand a system can absorb before that demand becomes troublesome.

Acceleration was one way to do that. Automation was another. Scale was another. Coordination was another. Skill was another. Somewhere along the way, I had taken Carù and Cova’s term and stretched it past what they meant.

The thing I had really become interested in was **capacity**.

The pattern in my head had changed with it. A system becomes saturated. So, we expand its capacity somehow. The new capacity changes what the system can support. Behavior, expectations, complexity, or scale grow around that new capability. Eventually the system encounters saturation again.

The second round of saturation may resemble the first, but it is no longer the same system anymore.

### Who Gets the Space?

This still left an obvious question: what occupies the capacity we create?

Sometimes the answer is not really ours to choose. 

We fill our time with work, errands, messages, meetings, maintenance, responsibilities, worries, and the administrative debris required to keep a contemporary life functioning. Then we get better at managing it. We automate something. Delegate something. Learn a faster method. Build better software. A task that once took a day takes an hour.

And somehow the afternoon does not become ours. If something that once took a day can now be done in an hour, an employer can reasonably ask for more of it. Faster communication creates expectations of faster response. Better tools allow one person to own more work. Productivity gains become the new baseline rather than a dividend paid out as leisure.

> Efficiency does not determine what happens to the time it saves. Power does.

There is a cultural layer too. Research by Silvia Bellezza, Neeru Paharia, and Anat Keinan found that, particularly in American contexts, visible busyness and lack of leisure can themselves signal status. A busy person is often inferred to be competent, ambitious, scarce, and in demand. 

That helps explain why externally legible usefulness can acquire a seriousness that internally chosen meaning does not. Earning, advancing, optimizing, maintaining, responding, producing, and improving are easy to defend as respectable uses of finite time. Wandering, tinkering, obsessing, playing, making, hosting, learning, and fucking around often feel as though they require justification.

It would be convenient to blame all of this on employers or productivity culture.

Unfortunately, I am perfectly capable of filling the space myself.

Give me an empty evening and I have a remarkable ability to turn it into a project. A bike ride becomes a regular ride. A regular ride becomes an event. The event needs a name. Then graphics. Then a website. Then people show up, which is wonderful, except now the ridiculous thing I made because I had some free time has expectations attached to it.

A hobby becomes a commitment. A curiosity becomes a project. A free weekend becomes something that requires a spreadsheet.

Nobody stole that capacity from me.

Often I am delighted by what replaced it.

That was enough to ruin the easy moral of the cycle. Some resaturation is bitter. Some is sweet. Sometimes the things that fill newly available capacity are exactly the things I wanted the capacity for.

So I could not simply conclude that saturation was the disease and emptiness the cure.

I do not actually want an empty life.

## Integration

The same pattern appeared somewhere much less personal.

Before I moved into my current corner of product development, GE Appliances had already transitioned from an NPI process to something called iNPI. The “i” stood for integrated.

The problem it was trying to solve was real. Different functions had their own requirements, checklists, and gate reviews. Engineering knew what engineering needed. Manufacturing knew what manufacturing needed. Quality knew what quality needed. Commercial teams had their own concerns. Everyone could successfully complete their local responsibilities and still produce a program failure because dependencies between those responsibilities had been missed.

Integration created a common picture.

Instead of scattered functional assessments, one tool could represent the program. Phases could be aligned. Dependencies became more visible. A program manager could look across the whole thing instead of trying to reconstruct reality from a collection of local truths.

It worked.

And because it worked, the system could support more.

Someone had to hold that integrated picture together, so more accountability converged on the program manager, even when that person lacked direct authority over everyone responsible for the work represented inside it.

Meanwhile, appliances themselves were becoming more complicated. Software became part of the product. Then connectivity. Cloud infrastructure. Mobile applications. More sophisticated interfaces. New technologies. More ambitious colors, materials, and finishes. More channels. More things that had to go right before launch.

The product became capable of doing more.

The organization became capable of coordinating more.

The assessment expanded with them.

Eventually, the mechanism designed to solve fragmentation became saturated by integration.

Now the process has been moving toward decomposition again: smaller functionally owned assessments, with the program manager still responsible for integration at the system level.

It would be easy to make this a parable about centralization inevitably giving way to decentralization. I do not think that is what happened.

The integrated system was not simply wrong. It solved something. The newer distributed structure is not simply a return to the old one, because the organization learned why independent silos failed in the first place.

The capacity expansion changed what the system could become.

Then the changed system encountered a new saturation.

That distinction mattered. Saturation was not necessarily evidence that an earlier intervention had failed. Sometimes a system became saturated precisely because the intervention succeeded.

## The Race

By this point I had the makings of one of those frameworks that explains everything.

That is usually when a framework becomes dangerous.

If washing machines, microwaves, corporate governance, modern work, and my hobbies could all be explained through saturation and capacity, I was either onto something or five minutes away from explaining nothing.

Bike racing made the model less tidy.

A criterium is a short circuit race in which a field of riders repeatedly accelerates, decelerates, compresses, stretches, and rearranges itself. At one moment, the road is saturated with handlebars and wheels. Thirty seconds later, the field can stretch into a thin line with open pavement everywhere.

Neither condition tells you whether things are going well.

A dense peloton can feel claustrophobic, but the riders around you provide a draft. Open road can mean you escaped in the winning breakaway. It can also mean everyone else rode away from you.

Acceleration is equally ambiguous. Sometimes it is the attack that wins the race. Sometimes it is a panicked response to something irrelevant. Sometimes the field accelerates and your choices are to respond or stop participating.

Deceleration is not the moral corrective. If a breakaway is failing, you do not undo excessive acceleration by slamming on the brakes. If the field feels crowded, you do not necessarily improve your situation by giving up the draft and riding alone.

There is no correct middle speed.

What matters is what the race is doing, what you are trying to accomplish, and what the moment requires.

Wajcman makes a similar objection to sweeping narratives of acceleration: some activities speed up while others slow down, and technologies take on different temporal meanings in different contexts. The useful question is not whether acceleration is categorically good or bad, but where it occurs and what it does there. 

Racing made that painfully literal.

Saturation, desaturation, acceleration, and deceleration were not moral categories. They were conditions. Their value depended on motive, agency, context, and objective.

Then racing complicated the model again.

A very fit rider can get away with being bad at racing.

They can sit in the wind too long. Brake too much into corners. Let gaps open. Chase attacks that never mattered. Spend enormous amounts of power repeatedly correcting mistakes.

If they are strong enough, they survive anyway.

A less fit but more experienced rider has fewer opportunities to waste energy. They carry speed. They anticipate where the field will compress. They understand which moves matter. They position themselves so that somebody else’s small mistake does not become their own massive acceleration twenty riders later.

One rider has more capacity.

The other may need less of it.

The strong rider’s fitness does more than compensate for bad tactics. It can suppress the feedback that would otherwise force the rider to improve them.

If every gap can be closed with brute force, a gap opening does not feel like much of a problem.

The same possibility exists elsewhere. A heroic program manager can keep a badly distributed process functioning. A sufficiently elaborate interface can make feature proliferation usable. A large washer can make a huge laundry backlog manageable. A sufficiently capable person can carry an absurd number of obligations without anything visibly breaking.

The system still works.

That does not mean the architecture is good.

Sometimes additional capacity solves the problem.

Sometimes it merely makes the problem survivable.

## What Saturation Knows

That distinction is where the model finally started doing something for me rather than merely describing something I had already noticed.

Saturation contains information.

When something exceeds our comfortable capacity, the instinctive response is often to increase capacity. The hamper is overflowing: bigger washer. The schedule is impossible: better productivity system. The employee is overloaded: automate more. The rider cannot stay attached: improve fitness.

Any of those may be correct.

But increasing capacity also changes the evidence.

The capable employee makes understaffing less visible. The fit rider makes poor positioning less costly. The large washer makes accumulation less consequential.

By removing the pressure, we may also remove the thing that was forcing us to examine why the pressure existed.

This does not make constraints virtuous. Some constraints are stupid and deserve to be destroyed. Sometimes the correct answer really is more people, more fitness, more automation, more storage, a larger machine.

The model cannot tell me which.

What it can do is delay the obvious answer long enough for me to wonder whether the thing presenting itself as a capacity problem might actually be a problem of architecture.

That distinction matters in product development because product developers are professional constraint removers. We encounter friction and reduce it. Effort and automate it. Waiting and shorten it. Insufficient capacity and enlarge it.

Usually, that is exactly the job.

But what happens if, before doing that, we treat the saturation as evidence?

What becomes visible if we resist fixing it for another minute?

Which brings me back to laundry.

## Laundry, Again

Start with the conventional problem definition.

People have dirty clothes. Dirty clothes accumulate. Washing them requires time, energy, water, detergent, attention, and equipment. Therefore, a better appliance processes that accumulation with less burden.

Make the drum larger. Make the cycle shorter. Sense the load. Dose automatically. Protect fabrics. Reduce noise. Use less water. Connect the machine. Notify me when the cycle ends. Adapt to more kinds of clothing.

Those are all legitimate design directions. Some are excellent improvements.

But if I resist treating the saturation itself as a fixed input, the problem begins changing shape.

Maybe part of a wardrobe is actually **inventory held against laundry lead time**.

A garment becomes unavailable after I wear it. It stays unavailable while it sits in a hamper, waits for a compatible load, gets washed, transferred, dried, folded, and returned to storage. I compensate for that latency by owning alternatives.

If my favorite shirt could move from *worn* to *ready* with almost no effort and delay, how many shirts would I actually need?

Now the wardrobe looks different.

Then the hamper looks different.

A hamper is a queue. We intentionally accumulate unfinished work until enough of it exists to justify batch processing. Then we resent the size of the task created by the queue.

What if the system eliminated the queue?

Take off today’s clothes. Put them somewhere. By tomorrow they have quietly returned to a wearable state. No load. No laundry day. Perhaps the machine becomes smaller because it no longer needs to absorb a week of deferred maintenance at once.

Then another assumption appears: why are clothes either **clean** or **dirty**?

A shirt worn for several hours may contain some sweat and odor without requiring the same treatment as a shirt covered in mud. Pants may need nothing more than airing. A sweater may be perfectly fine. Yet our existing household system often provides two destinations—closet or hamper—and crossing into the second frequently triggers water, detergent, agitation, heat, and drying.

Perhaps there should be a third state.

Not clean. Not dirty.

Worn.

Maybe an entirely different appliance belongs there: airflow, moisture management, steaming, targeted odor treatment, spot cleaning, or whatever combination of technologies actually proves useful. Full washing becomes periodic maintenance rather than the automatic consequence of having worn something.

And if routine garment care becomes lighter, quieter, and less infrastructure-intensive, perhaps it does not belong in the laundry room.

What if the closet were partly the appliance?

Hang up the shirt you wore today. Overnight, the storage environment determines whether it needs nothing, airing, moisture removal, wrinkle release, or some other treatment. The conventional washer survives for underwear, linens, genuine dirt, and periodic deep cleaning, but it stops being the only meaningful route from *worn* back to *ready*.

Once that boundary moves, the unit of work changes too.

Washing machines optimize a load. Capacity is measured in pounds or cubic feet. Efficiency is discussed per cycle.

But I rarely care about a load.

I care that my cycling kit is clean tomorrow. I care that this shirt is wearable tonight. A parent cares that the school uniform is ready in the morning.

What if laundry capacity were measured in **time-to-next-wear** instead of drum volume?

A machine that holds twice as much clothing at once has greater conventional capacity. Another system might keep the garments a household actually wants continuously available with almost no human attention. Which system is more capable?

And once that question is allowed, the possibilities start multiplying faster than I can responsibly evaluate them.

What if automation treated garments individually enough that sorting largely disappeared?

What if clothing participated in its own maintenance through materials, construction, better care information, replaceable high-wear areas, or designs intended for localized treatment?

What if the best laundry innovation came from a textile company rather than an appliance company?

What if storage, refreshing, cleaning, drying, and wardrobe management were one system instead of separate categories?

What if the useful performance metric were not how much clothing a machine could process at once, but how reliably a relatively small set of clothing stayed available?

And then the question that still feels strangest to me:

What if one measure of an excellent laundry system were that it allowed a household to comfortably own **less clothing**?

Not because minimalism is morally superior. Not because everyone should own seven identical shirts. Simply because technology had reduced the redundancy required to keep the clothing we actually like available.

Imagine putting that on the box.

Not *5.8 cubic feet of mega capacity.*

*Keeps a five-day wardrobe continuously available.*

I have no idea whether anybody would buy it.

Several of these ideas may be thermodynamically stupid. Some may consume more energy. Some may shorten garment life. Some may be irritating in practice. Families have wildly different requirements. Batch washing may survive every one of these questions and remain overwhelmingly superior.

The full-size washing machine may emerge from this thought experiment almost unchanged.

That is not a failure.

The value is that I can now see how many assumptions were packed into the original problem before anyone began solving it.

The wardrobe became inventory.

The hamper became a queue.

Dirty became an unnecessarily broad state.

The closet became part of the clothing-care system.

The load stopped being the only sensible unit of work.

Capacity stopped meaning cubic feet.

Eventually the washing machine itself stopped looking like the inevitable center of laundry innovation.

Years ago, I dismissed Bell and Kaye because they did not help me build a better microwave.

I think I misunderstood what kind of help they were offering.

They were not handing me another feature.

They were giving me permission to redraw the problem.

## Capacity

Carù and Cova gave me the terms that started this whole line of thought, but somewhere along the way the model changed.

Acceleration became only one way to create capacity. And capacity turned out not to be an empty space waiting passively for us to decide what to do with it. Increasing capacity changes what a system can support. That can enable new possibilities, new expectations, new complexity, new commitments, and eventually new forms of saturation.

I am still not convinced that the answer to saturation is desaturation, or that acceleration should ultimately give way to slowing down. That conclusion feels too clean for the systems I actually inhabit.

Sometimes I want the packed calendar. Sometimes a ridiculous hobby deserves to become a commitment. Sometimes the breakaway requires an acceleration that is objectively unreasonable and tactically correct.

Sometimes the bigger washer is the right machine.

I do not know whether this cycle is an inevitable human condition, a cultural habit, an economic consequence, or merely a pattern that appears often enough to be useful. I am increasingly comfortable not asking it to answer that question.

What it has changed is what happens immediately before I reach for more capacity.

I want to know what produced the saturation, who is driving it, and what the system is actually trying to accomplish. I want to know whether the next increase in capacity will remove a burden, enable something worth doing, redistribute the burden somewhere more appropriate, or simply allow the same architecture to grow larger before it complains again.

Most of the time, somebody eventually needs an answer. A product has to ship. A project has to move. A gap has to be closed. A pile of clothes has to get clean.

I am still going to solve capacity problems.

I just want to understand what the saturation was trying to tell me before I make it disappear.
