### I. The Open Category

At an alleycat race, there are usually at least two ways to enter.

The fixed-gear category belongs to riders whose pedals never stop turning. The open category contains almost everything else: road bikes, mountain bikes, single-speeds, commuters, whatever someone thinks might get them across the city fastest.

I entered through the open category.

> **PERSONAL ANECDOTE NEEDED:** Open with one specific alleycat. Describe the start location, the riders, the bikes, the manifest, and your position among them. Who did you know? What made you feel welcomed? What made you feel conspicuously separate? Include one small sensory detail—a sound, gesture, piece of clothing, bike, joke, or ritual—that made the culture feel established before you arrived.

I am a slightly above-average cyclist, at least by the standards of people who voluntarily spend too much of their lives riding bicycles. In a conventional race, fitness generally reveals itself with some cruelty. Stronger riders produce more power, survive longer, and eventually ride away.

An alleycat measures something less pure.

It requires fitness, but also memory, navigation, local knowledge, risk tolerance, and the ability to make good decisions while your heart rate is high. The organizer gives you a collection of checkpoints and perhaps a few rules. What the organizer does not give you is a route.

The objective is clear. The timeline is urgent. The process is yours.

This is, perhaps unsurprisingly, where I tend to do my best work.

I obsess over maps. I compare routes. I think about sequences, bottlenecks, optionality, and the cost of reversing a bad decision. I enjoy the possibility that the most obvious path may not be the best one. An alleycat allows me to combine adequate legs with a brain that has spent much of its life looking for hidden systems.

It resembles the work I love at FirstBuild. There is a destination, a deadline, and no reliable map between them. Someone may tell you what outcome matters, but rarely how to reach it. The best route often requires questioning the rules, conventions, or best practices that made the problem appear orderly in the first place.

Give me a prescribed process, and I may resist it.

Give me an ambiguous problem already in motion, and I become strangely comfortable.

Alleycats eventually drew me toward the fixed-gear riders who gathered around them. They did not make me one of those riders. They merely gave me an entrance into their world.

I entered through the open category.

In retrospect, that may describe more than the race.

### II. The Houses I Almost Joined

Some of the communities that have influenced me most are those I have never felt able to claim.

Libertarianism gave language to my suspicion of authority. Daoism gave language to my suspicion of language itself—the tendency of names and categories to harden around experiences they can never completely contain. Alleycats brought me into proximity with fixed-gear culture, a community I admire, spend time around, and still hesitate to describe as mine.

The same pattern appears elsewhere.

I have loved learning without ever feeling at home in academia. I loved baseball without becoming the collegiate athlete I thought I could be. I work inside one of the largest appliance corporations in the world, but at one of its strangest and least conventionally corporate edges. I have participated in electoral reform while becoming progressively less able to describe my politics through the vocabulary of elections.

These things are not equivalent. A political philosophy is not a workplace. A cycling subculture is not a religion. A college baseball team is not a theory of social organization.

The similarity lies in my posture toward them.

I enter sincerely. I learn the customs. I become attached to certain people, practices, and principles. I begin to recognize what outsiders misunderstand. I may even feel protective when the community is reduced to its least flattering stereotype.

But I also notice the assumptions that insiders stop seeing. I resist the small acts of loyalty through which participation becomes identity. I become uncomfortable when affection is treated as consent to the whole arrangement.

I have often been close enough to defend a community against outsiders while remaining too far outside to speak on its behalf.

There are differences between agreeing with something, being influenced by it, participating in it, belonging to it, and identifying as it. We tend to collapse them because clean identities are socially useful. They tell other people where to place us. They allow communities to recognize allies and opponents. They make complicated people legible.

But the things that shape us do not always fit inside the identities we are willing to claim.

Some of the strongest influences in my life have been houses I could enter but never convince myself to own.

### III. A Principle Simple Enough to Live Inside

Libertarianism first appealed to me because coercion seemed like a problem simple enough to name.

People should generally be left alone.

It is difficult to look seriously at the history of governments and conclude that concentrated political power deserves the benefit of the doubt. War, imprisonment, segregation, displacement, redlining, surveillance, corruption, and legally protected inequity are not accidental blemishes on an otherwise neutral institution. They are among the things states have repeatedly been built to accomplish.

The Non-Aggression Principle offered a clean moral response: no person or institution should initiate force against another.

There was something exhilarating in its simplicity.

> **PERSONAL ANECDOTE NEEDED:** Describe when libertarianism first became personally compelling. Was there a book, conversation, online community, election, class, news event, or frustration that made the principle click? How old were you? What did the label allow you to say about yourself?

A single principle appeared capable of replacing the selective morality of partisan politics. It did not matter whether the aggression came from the left or the right, from a popular administration or an unpopular one. If coercion was wrong, it remained wrong even when carried out by people who claimed good intentions.

This felt more honest than politics as I understood it.

It also made the world appear unusually legible. Identify the aggressor. Protect the individual. Dismantle the institution doing the coercing. Leave people free to make their own choices.

For a while, the house seemed complete.

Its walls were made of individual liberty. Its foundation was private choice. The state stood outside as the great intruder, forever entering rooms where it had not been invited.

But simple principles often remain simple because they define complexity as someone else’s problem.

The Non-Aggression Principle could easily identify a soldier, police officer, tax collector, or censor. It was less decisive when confronted with accumulated power, dependency, inherited disadvantage, and relationships that were voluntary only in the most formal sense.

It could ask whether force had been initiated.

It had more trouble asking who possessed the realistic ability to refuse.

The house had seemed simple because I had not yet seen all its rooms.

### IV. Discovering Who Built the Market

The distinction between government and business began to collapse as I encountered libertarians who were less interested in defending capitalism than in examining how capitalism had been constructed.

Charles Johnson’s “Markets Freed from Capitalism.” Gary Chartier’s “Advocates of Freed Markets Should Oppose Capitalism.” Benjamin Tucker. Kevin Carson. Later, the Center for a Stateless Society and other corners of left-libertarian thought.

They did not ask me to abandon my suspicion of the state. They asked me to apply it more consistently.

The market presented in conventional libertarianism was often described as though it were a natural landscape that appeared wherever government withdrew. But actual markets were built through legal arrangements, property regimes, infrastructure choices, subsidies, monopoly protections, selective enforcement, and the socialization of private risk.

Large corporations and large states did not merely compete for power. They frequently produced one another.

Government created captive markets and barriers to entry. Businesses converted economic dominance into political privilege. Regulations designed or advertised as public protections could become tools through which incumbents made competition more expensive. Public institutions absorbed costs while private institutions retained ownership of the returns.

The supposed enemies were often partners.

Centralization sucked, whether its logo appeared on a government seal or the side of an office tower.

This realization did not make me less concerned with coercion. It made me less willing to recognize coercion only when exercised by the state.

It also changed my relationship to the political identity that had brought me there. I still recognized its founding intuition. I still believed that authority required justification and that voluntary arrangements were generally preferable to imposed ones. But I could no longer accept a framework in which private power became legitimate merely by being private.

The libertarian house contained a room full of corporate power that many of its residents insisted belonged to another building.

The more closely I examined the architecture, the less certain I became that I lived there.

### V. No Permanent Political Address

My understanding of domination widened.

The state was not the only institution capable of limiting human agency. Employers, landlords, monopolies, inherited wealth, racial hierarchies, social expectations, and material dependency could all shape which choices were realistically available.

An agreement can be technically voluntary while one party has almost no meaningful ability to reject it.

This did not erase the difference between violence and bargaining, or between government and private association. It did make those distinctions less capable of resolving every question on their own.

Bleeding Heart Libertarians and C4SS offered spaces where liberty was discussed alongside vulnerability, inequality, and social justice. They were attempts—imperfect, disputed, occasionally contradictory—to preserve a concern for autonomy without pretending that all domination arrives through legislation.

I became harder to classify.

To some libertarians, broadening the critique of power looked like abandoning liberty for collectivism. To some progressives, maintaining a suspicion of state power looked naïve or obstructive. To conservatives, my hostility toward entrenched corporate privilege and inherited hierarchy placed me elsewhere again.

Eventually, I entered formal political advocacy through Rank the Vote Kentucky, an organization promoting ranked-choice voting.

Include the friendship with the Ranked Choice Voting Resource Center contributor only where it humanizes the experience rather than adding credentials.

Ranked-choice voting appealed to me partly because it challenged the forced binary of American politics. It offered voters more room to express preference without treating every election as a choice between loyalty and betrayal.

But political reform still required political performance. Coalitions needed messages. Organizations needed positions. People wanted to know which side we were helping, which side we were hurting, and whether changing the mechanism would produce the outcomes they already preferred.

I became increasingly politically homeless.

Not politically indifferent. Not neutral. Not free of values.

Homeless.

I could often understand the fears, experiences, or moral intuitions motivating people on opposing sides. This rarely made either side trust me more.

Most tribes know what to do with an enemy. Enemies clarify the boundary. They reinforce the story the group tells about itself.

A person who understands both sides is harder to classify.

Empathy at the intersection can appear less principled than allegiance at either pole. Refusing the dichotomy is interpreted as an inability to choose, even when the dichotomy itself is what deserves to be questioned.

### VI. Hospitality

Political philosophy initially taught me to imagine freedom as the ability to be left alone.

Life made that definition increasingly difficult to sustain.

I have traveled through China, Japan, and Portugal as someone dependent on systems of meaning I did not create. Language, etiquette, transportation, hospitality, and ordinary social competence all belonged more naturally to other people.

> **PERSONAL ANECDOTE NEEDED:** Choose one small travel moment in which you were dependent on another person’s knowledge or generosity. Avoid making the anecdote a sweeping claim about the country. Focus on what it felt like to lack fluency and to be received anyway.

Travel makes guesthood literal.

A guest does not arrive sovereign. A guest depends upon a door being opened, directions being offered, mistakes being forgiven, and unfamiliar needs being made intelligible. What feels like personal competence at home can disappear as soon as the context changes.

The experience exposes self-sufficiency as partly environmental.

We are capable because other people maintain roads, teach languages, grow food, create norms, share knowledge, and perform the invisible labor that allows our choices to mean anything. Even our independence is scaffolded by relationships.

My friendships complicated my politics in similar ways. Real people rarely embody internally consistent ideologies. Their beliefs emerge through race, class, gender, work, family, religion, geography, injury, care, opportunity, and the particular collection of accidents through which a life takes shape.

> **PERSONAL ANECDOTE NEEDED:** Include one friendship or cluster of friendships that challenged your political categories. The point is not to reveal anyone’s private politics, but to show how affection and proximity made ideological simplification feel inadequate.

The abstract individual of libertarian theory began to look lonely.

Freedom was not merely the absence of interference. It was also the presence of capacity: trust, shared resources, reciprocal responsibility, common infrastructure, knowledge, and care.

We do not become free by escaping every obligation to one another.

We become capable through relationships that none of us could create alone.

A guest’s freedom exists partly because someone has opened a door.

### VII. The Art of Not Forcing

Daoism gave me language for something I had been circling without being able to name.

Or perhaps it gave me permission to stop naming it so aggressively.

The *Dao De Jing* is suspicious of the human tendency to force reality into categories and then forget that we created them. Names are useful, but they are not the things they name. Rules can create order, but an obsession with control can produce the disorder it was designed to prevent.

*Wu wei* is sometimes translated as non-action, which makes it sound like passivity. I understand it more as action without compulsive forcing: responding to the conditions that exist rather than continuously punishing them for failing to match the conditions we imagined.

This is not an argument against intention.

It is an argument against mistaking intention for reality.

Contemporary identity frequently demands declarations.

You are or are not a libertarian.

You are or are not a Daoist.

You are or are not part of fixed-gear culture.

You belong or you do not.

These declarations simplify coordination. They help us find communities and establish trust. They can also transform partial truths into total allegiances.

My own experience has been less clean.

I can be influenced by a philosophy without becoming its adherent. I can participate in a community without feeling entitled to represent it. I can defend a group against caricature while retaining serious criticisms of what happens inside it.

Perhaps my inability to settle into identities has sometimes been a failure of commitment. But perhaps some identities demand more certainty than experience can honestly provide.

Daoism does not resolve this conflict by identifying the correct side. It questions the need to divide the world so confidently in the first place.

The intersection may not be an indecisive midpoint between two complete positions.

It may be where their incompleteness becomes visible.

### VIII. The Bicycle I Changed Back

Alleycats brought me into closer contact with fixed-gear riders.

The race categories made the distinction explicit. Fixed riders competed against fixed riders. The open category gave everyone else a legitimate entrance.

Through those races, I began spending more time with people who voluntarily chose to ride bicycles with one gear and no ability to coast. I wanted to understand why.

The appeal became clearer with proximity.

A fixed-gear bicycle is mechanically simple and physically demanding. The drivetrain remains in direct conversation with the rider. As the rear wheel turns, the pedals turn. Momentum is never entirely surrendered to the machine. The limitation creates rhythm, technique, and a form of attention that geared bicycles do not require in quite the same way.

There is also culture around it: messenger history, urban competence, aesthetic coherence, risk, skill, and the pride of mastering a machine whose limitations are deliberate.

Eventually, I bought a State Black Label.

> **PERSONAL ANECDOTE NEEDED:** Describe why you chose the bike, your early fixed-gear rides, and the moment or accumulation of moments that made you decide it was not fully for you. What felt compelling? What felt physically, mentally, or philosophically wrong?

I now ride it single-speed, with a freewheel and a brake.

I came close enough to fixed-gear culture to buy the bicycle, then changed it at precisely the points where belonging would have required me to ignore my own judgment.

That sounds like a joke about my failure to commit. Perhaps it is.

But I did not reject the people or the ideas that drew me toward the bike. I learned why someone would choose the constraint. I became more capable of seeing what outsiders miss when they reduce fixed-gear riders to reckless hipsters running red lights.

I simply did not become one of them.

My relationship with alleycats is different. The race itself fits me. I understand its problem structure almost instinctively. I can be competitive because it rewards the whole strange arrangement of my abilities rather than isolating cycling fitness from everything else I know how to do.

I may be more at home solving the ritual than I am within the culture surrounding it.

I can be good at the race without becoming one of its people.

### IX. The Cost of Keeping One Hand on the Door

There is an appealing version of this story in which I am simply too independent for institutions and too intellectually honest for labels.

I do not trust that version.

Skepticism may protect principle. It can also protect the ego.

I was a poor college student despite loving knowledge. It is tempting to explain this through my alienation from academic systems: I cared about learning, not grades; ideas, not credentials; understanding, not the performance of understanding.

Some of that may be true.

It may also be true that I resisted the repetitive discipline through which curiosity becomes mastery.

I loved baseball. I did not become the collegiate athlete I imagined.

Years later, cycling gave me another path into competition, but I approached formal racing cautiously. Perhaps collegiate athletics taught me something useful about institutions, identity, and performance. Perhaps it merely left me afraid to discover the limits of my ability again.

Corporate life produces a similar contradiction.

I work within GE Appliances, but at FirstBuild: a comparatively small, ambiguous, experimental environment where convention is negotiable and no one always knows the correct route. It is corporate enough to build real products, but porous enough to challenge how products are normally built.

I found a place near the institution’s edge.

There is humility in calling myself a guest. There can also be avoidance.

Did I remain outside because I saw institutions clearly, or did I learn to emphasize their failures because residence demanded more of me than I wanted to give?

Did skepticism preserve my principles, or protect me from judgment?

Did I avoid false identities, or the vulnerability required by any durable one?

I do not know.

I suspect the answer changes from one house to another.

### X. Residents Who Have Forgotten

I may live this condition more intensely than most.

But I doubt it belongs to me.

There is the employee who contributes for decades without ever feeling that the company’s identity is their own. The believer who cannot accept every doctrine of the religion that taught them how to pray. The child who loves their family while feeling strangely peripheral within it. The transplant who has lived in a city for twenty years and still refers to somewhere else as home.

There is the athlete who loves the game but distrusts the structures surrounding it. The citizen who votes without finding themselves represented by a party. The friend welcomed into a group whose oldest stories occurred before they arrived.

We learn to hide these distances because belonging is supposed to feel complete.

We say “we” and hope the word closes the gap.

Even the people who appear entirely at home entered after the community began. They inherited its language, rituals, disputes, and institutions. They did not create most of what they defend. Their belonging depends partly on other people continuing to recognize them as belonging.

Eventually they will leave, age out, change, be displaced, or die.

Perhaps the resident is also a guest—only comfortable enough to have forgotten.

This does not mean belonging is false.

It means belonging was never the same thing as ownership.

### XI. Guests All the Way Down

The pattern repeats at every scale.

We are guests in cultures whose languages and symbols were formed before us.

We are guests in institutions whose histories we enter midway through.

We are guests in cities built by strangers and maintained by people we may never meet.

We are guests in bodies we did not design, whose abilities change without our consent, and which we are permitted to inhabit only temporarily.

We are guests in historical moments we did not choose.

We are guests on a planet we inherited from people who are gone and will leave to people whose names we will never know.

We are guests in time itself.

The universe was not waiting for us. We arrive during a story already in progress and depart before it is finished.

This could sound like the most complete form of alienation: no permanent home, no stable identity, no possession that survives long enough to justify the word.

But guesthood contains another feeling.

Gratitude.

To be a guest is to have been admitted somewhere, however briefly.

We eat food we did not grow. We speak words we did not invent. We travel roads we did not build. We inherit stories, tools, rituals, errors, and unfinished projects. We experience a world assembled through countless acts of labor and care that occurred before our arrival.

I spent years treating guesthood as evidence that I had failed to find a home.

Perhaps permanence was never available to anyone.

We arrive midway through.

We depend on arrangements we did not create.

We alter them by participating.

We leave before the story ends.

### XII. What Kind of Guest?

The answer cannot be permanent detachment.

A guest who refuses every obligation is not liberated. They are merely consuming someone else’s hospitality.

A good guest pays attention.

They learn the customs without mistaking them for natural law. They accept generosity without converting it into entitlement. They contribute. They help maintain the place. They question rules that cause harm without assuming that everything unfamiliar is foolish.

They help with the dishes.

They do not confuse affection with ownership.

They remember that other guests will arrive after them.

Perhaps this is how I want to belong: not as an owner, partisan, or permanent resident, but as someone temporarily responsible.

To participate without surrendering judgment.

To love a community without believing it innocent.

To help build institutions without allowing their survival to become more important than their purpose.

To stay without pretending that staying makes the place mine.

> **PERSONAL ANECDOTE NEEDED:** Return to the alleycat from the opening. Describe the finish or dispersal. Who arrived? What state were people in? What happened to the temporary community once the race was over? Echo one image or phrase from the first section.

An alleycat briefly reorganizes the city.

Riders move between checkpoints by routes no organizer prescribes. Some make brilliant decisions. Some choose badly. Some get lost. Rules are interpreted, tested, and occasionally ignored. For a few hours, people use the city differently than it was designed to be used.

Then they finish.

The manifests are turned in. Riders argue about routes, compare mistakes, and recover together. Eventually the gathering dissolves back into ordinary traffic.

No one owns the course because there was never one course to own.

I entered through the open category. I was welcomed into a culture that did not have to become mine in order to change me.

I once thought freedom meant preserving the ability to leave. I still believe the door matters. But perhaps the harder task is learning how to stay without pretending to possess: to participate without surrendering judgment, to love without demanding permanence, and to care for places that will continue after us.

We are all guests.

The question is whether the places we pass through will be more hospitable because we were here.
